## Series A · Direction Stack protocols (10 posts · one per protocol)

### POST 01 · Protocol 01 · Claw Hands
> Most founder LinkedIn photos have one thing in common at the hands.

> The fingers are spread. Tension is visible at the knuckles. The viewer reads it before they read the face.

> The fix is not a different photographer. The fix is one direction: give the hands a task.

> Pocket. Lapel. A folded element at the wrist. The hand stops performing and starts deploying.

> A founder photo is read in 200 milliseconds. The hands move first.

### POST 02 · Protocol 02 · Locked Shoulders
> Your shoulders in your LinkedIn photo are doing one of two things.

> Defensive (elevated, braced toward the camera) or open (dropped, rotated 12 degrees off-axis).

> The first reads as "I'm being photographed." The second reads as "I'm in the room."

> Investors read defensive shoulders before they read your bio. The deck doesn't recover what the photo already lost.

### POST 03 · Protocol 03 · Squared to Camera
> The default founder photo: body fully square to the lens.

> Reads as a yearbook portrait. Even on a $300 headshot session.

> Rotation is the architecture. 35 degrees off-axis, weight on one leg. The same person becomes a deliberate person.

> One angle. The room shifts.

### POST 04 · Protocol 04 · Pinned Arms
> Arms pinned to the torso make a founder look smaller than the business they run.

> Negative space between arm and body is not optional. It's the silhouette.

> Watch the next CEO press portrait you scroll past. Count the negative space. The ones who look in command have more of it.

### POST 05 · Protocol 05 · Soft Jawline
> The single highest-yield correction in founder photography is the jawline.

> Chin pulled back collapses the jaw into the neck. Reads as uncertain at thumbnail size.

> Chin pushed forward and slightly down defines the jaw. Reads as decisive even on a 60-pixel avatar.

> One direction. Re-frames the rest of the face.

### POST 06 · Protocol 06 · Spinal Collapse
> The cost of a closed chest in your founder photo is paid on every deck slide it appears in.

> Upper spine rounded forward, shoulders rolled in, neck shortened. Reads as fatigue.

> Spine extended, chest open, neck long. Reads as authority.

> The body is the deck before the slides are the deck.

### POST 07 · Protocol 07 · Forced Smile
> The smile in most founder photos arrives before the eyes.

> Mechanical, performed, off by 200 milliseconds. The viewer can't name what's wrong, but the post doesn't get the like.

> Eyes commit first, smile follows. Always in that order. The photographer's job is to wait for the order to be right.

### POST 08 · Protocol 08 · Transition Freeze
> The strongest founder portraits are not poses.

> They're the in-between. The breath after the laugh. The half-second before the next thought. The frame where the subject didn't know it was the frame.

> Direction is not "hold this pose." Direction is creating the space where the in-between can happen and being ready to catch it.

### POST 09 · Protocol 09 · Good Mechanics, Zero Presence
> Technically perfect founder portraits sometimes feel like stock photos of a founder.

> Pose is right. Lighting is right. Retouch is right. Eyes are not present.

> Mechanics get you to a polished frame. Mechanics alone don't get you a frame anyone wants to read.

> The 10th protocol is the only one that's not a body part. It's whether the person showed up.

### POST 10 · Protocol 10 · Full Psychological Shutdown
> The hardest correction is when a founder retreats from the lens.

> Eyes have gone blank. The face is there. The person is somewhere else. Most photographers don't know how to bring them back; the shoot wraps with empty frames.

> Direction is not just choreography. It's the conversation that pulls the person back to this side of the camera. The 90-second on-set opener is what determines whether the day produces a frame at all.

## Series B · Operator identity + market POV (10 posts)

### POST 11 · The visual operating level
> Your business has an operating level. Your visual presence has one too.

> When the second is below the first, your investors, partners, and press are paying micro-friction every time they look you up. You don't see the cost. They do.

> The fix is not a hobbyist photo session. The fix is a directed session that runs a methodology on set. That's an operations problem, not an art problem.

### POST 12 · The headshot vs the founder portrait
> A headshot is a deliverable. A founder portrait is a positioning move.

> Headshots are commodity. Every photographer in LA shoots them. The deliverable is photos.

> Founder portraits are infrastructure. The deliverable is what your investor sees first when they pull up your profile.

> Different services. Different prices. Different photographers.

### POST 13 · Why methodology is the moat
> Talent is the entry requirement. Not the differentiator.

> Every working photographer in LA can take a technically sound photograph. The differentiator is the system: the diagnostic that runs on set, the protocol that gets named and corrected, the consistency across every frame in the gallery.

> Methodology is what a client can't see when they're shopping. It's what makes the photos work.

### POST 14 · The 200-millisecond test
> Your LinkedIn photo is processed in 200 milliseconds.

> That's faster than the viewer reads your headline.

> Whatever the photo signals, it signals before any words land. If the signal is wrong, every word after fights uphill.

> The 200-millisecond test is the founder portrait's only test.

### POST 15 · From engineering to operating
> I spent four years on the floor of a data center. Engineering is what taught me to run photography like a system, not a craft.

> Mechanical posture is a diagnostic. Psychological presence is a diagnostic. The shoot is the implementation. The retouch is the QA.

> Most founder portraits fail not because the photographer was bad but because there was no diagnostic running on set.

### POST 16 · What you're paying for
> When you book a $1,500 founder portrait session, you're not paying for 45 minutes of camera time.

> You're paying for the diagnostic that ran before, the direction that runs during, and the editorial standard that runs after.

> The 45 minutes is the smallest part of the work.

### POST 17 · The cost of the wrong photo
> A wrong founder photo on a Series A press cycle has a quantifiable cost.

> Lower click-through on every press release. Lower conversion on every "About" page hit. Higher friction on every cold-introduction email forwarded with a screenshot.

> The fix is one session. The cost of the unfix compounds quarterly.

### POST 18 · The team page reveal
> Look at the team page of any company you've considered partnering with.

> Read the inconsistency. Different shoots, different lighting, different registers. Mismatched headshots from different years stacked in a grid.

> The team page is reading you before you read it. The mismatch reads as a mismatch in the company.

### POST 19 · The "good enough" trap
> "Good enough" founder photos cost more than premium founder photos in the long run.

> Each "good enough" deployment compounds. The press cycle. The deck. The podcast cover. The investor follow-up email. Every one of them carries a frame that's reading slightly wrong.

> A premium session is a once-a-year cost. The "good enough" trap is a daily one.

### POST 20 · Photo as deployment, not artifact
> The frame from the session is not the deliverable.

> The deployment is the deliverable. Where the photo lands. What it does there. How it reads when scrolled past on a Tuesday morning.

> A photographer who doesn't think about deployment is a photographer who hands you frames you have to figure out where to put.

## Series C · Behind the scenes + craft (10 posts)

### POST 21 · The 90-second on-set opener
> The first 90 seconds of a founder portrait session decide the outcome of the next 45 minutes.

> Address the insecurity in the first question. Frame the next 45 minutes. Run the 7-second physical warm-up. Direct the first pose with rhythm.

> Subjects who are warm by minute 2 produce keepers from minute 5. Subjects who aren't warm until minute 30 produce 30 minutes of unusable frames.

### POST 22 · Why I light the way I light
> A single soft key light flatters the face. It also flattens the frame.

> The signature SNIPED setup is key plus rim. The rim is the silhouette. Without it, dark suits dissolve into dark backgrounds and the subject melts into the frame.

> One small modifier behind the subject. Most photographers underuse it. It's the difference between a portrait and a documented person.

### POST 23 · On retouch
> Clinical retouch protects pore detail.

> The mistake is the smoothed-skin look that reads as a magazine ad from 2008. SNIPED's standard is unified complexion, equalized color casts, preserved texture.

> If the retouch removes the skin, the photo failed before retouching started.

### POST 24 · The locked palette
> Wardrobe gets locked before the session.

> Monochromatic. One tonal family. No accidents. The viewer's eye reads color blocks before it reads pose.

> Most founder photos lose because the wardrobe was assembled, not chosen. A directed session starts with a locked palette and never deviates.

### POST 25 · One thing
> The strongest founder portraits have one deliberate element.

> One prop. One color. One gesture. One piece of context the standard photographer would never bother to add.

> The other 99% of the frame is competent. The 1% carries the image.

### POST 26 · Why I shoot in 45 minutes
> The Reset is 45 minutes on camera.

> Past minute 50, the subject is fatigued. Past minute 60, posing collapses. The strongest frames live between minutes 20 and 40.

> The discipline of finishing on time is the operator move. Most shoots that go long do so to compensate for upstream failures, not to capture more.

### POST 27 · Direction is rhythm
> Watch a portrait session and listen to the photographer.

> Flat delivery: "Can you put your hand here, can you put your shoulder here, can you put your foot here." The subject reads uncertainty.

> Rhythmic delivery: "Hand up, hold, eyes here, breathe, frame." The subject reads competence and relaxes.

> Direction is rhythm before it's content.

### POST 28 · The eyes follow the camera, not the photographer
> The hardest direction is keeping the camera off your face when the subject is uncertain.

> They read your face. Not the lens. If the camera is in front of your face, they're reading nothing.

> Direct with the camera at your hip. Look at the subject. Confirm the hits. Pull the camera up only when the frame is right.

### POST 29 · The hero image discipline
> A 60-image gallery dilutes its own strongest frames.

> A 20-image gallery curated to the protocol corrections elevates them.

> The discipline is decisive culling. 20 images that all support the visual claim is a stronger deliverable than 60 that include 40 the photographer wasn't sure about.

### POST 30 · The shoot is one of three things
> A photo session is three things, in order:

> Pre-production (decides 80% of the outcome). On-set direction (decides 15%). Retouch (decides 5%).

> The session that fails was usually pre-produced wrong. By the time the camera comes out, the result is mostly decided. The on-set work refines what was already locked.

> The pre-shoot brief is not a formality. It's the deliverable.

## Series D · Cultural infrastructure + engineer-as-operator (10 posts · v1.1 addition)

These posts widen the bank beyond protocol-named diagnostic content. They speak to the operator-not-photographer framing (Spine v1.1 C3), the engineer integration (C4), and the cultural documentation thesis (C5). Mix into the rotation; do not bunch.

### POST 31 · Not a photographer
> Most of what I do isn't taking photos.

> Most of what I do is running a system. Pre-shoot diagnostics. On-set protocol direction. Editorial standard in post. Pixieset upsell mechanics. The 90-second warm-up that decides whether the next 45 minutes produce keepers or noise.

> The photo is the deliverable. The system is the product.

### POST 32 · The engineer's frame
> Spent four years on the floor of a data center before this became the work.

> What you learn there: every failure has a root cause, every system has a load-bearing component, every uptime number is a discipline that compounds across years.

> When I show up to a portrait session, I run a 10-protocol diagnostic before the camera comes up. That's not photography habit. That's engineering habit.

### POST 33 · The visual operating level
> Your business has an operating level. Your visual presence has one too.

> Most founders I speak to never thought about this explicitly. The investor deck is calibrated. The pricing is calibrated. The hiring bar is calibrated. The photo on LinkedIn is from a wedding three years ago.

> The visual operating level is the cheapest layer to upgrade and the most expensive layer to ignore.

### POST 34 · What documentation looks like (cultural infrastructure POV)
> Spent Sunday at a service in South LA documenting an institution that's been there longer than anyone reading this has been working.

> Three generations in the frame. The bishop in his element. The choir in fluorescent-Sunday-light. Forty families I'll see again at the banquet in June.

> The work that compounds is the work nobody else is doing seriously. This is one of those rooms.

### POST 35 · The body of work as the moat
> AI will commodify generic photography in three to five years.

> What AI cannot commodify: ten years of in-person presence in specific cultural environments with editorial-grade methodology applied to the documentation. The archive is the moat.

> Photographers building bodies of work will compound. Photographers chasing commission jobs will be priced out by the next generation of generative tools.

### POST 36 · Why I shoot at events for "free"
> Free is the wrong word.

> A free event coverage is paid in introductions. The artist's manager. The promoter's marketing director. The brand's sponsor contact. Each one of those is worth more than the day rate I'd have charged.

> The discipline is naming the introduction in writing before the camera comes out. Without the named return, free becomes the path to no growth.

### POST 37 · The three engines
> SNIPED Media runs three engines simultaneously.

> Revenue Engine: cold LinkedIn DMs to LA founders, discovery call, the Reset at $1,500. Predictable, fast, fund-the-business work.

> Audience Engine: the LinkedIn POV, the Instagram grid, the cultural-gravity layer that makes the cold DM convert higher. What someone sees when they scroll.

> Reputation Engine: documenting the institutions, the artists, the promoters, the rooms nobody else is shooting seriously. What someone says about SNIPED when SNIPED isn't in the room. The body of work that becomes the position five and ten years out.

> Most photographers run only the first one and wonder why the work feels disposable. The other two are the engines that don't.

### POST 38 · Operator coded vs artist coded
> There are two types of working photographers in LA.

> Artist-coded: the work is the identity. Pricing is uncomfortable. Systems feel inauthentic. Free shoots accumulate.

> Operator-coded: the work is the deliverable. Pricing is the price. Systems are the product. Free shoots are filtered for return.

> Both produce great photos sometimes. Only one produces a sustainable business.

### POST 39 · The studio is wherever I'm working
> Don't have a fixed studio space right now.

> Run sessions at three rotating locations: a DTLA studio I share access at, a mentor's space, occasional Peerspace bookings.

> Not a constraint. A feature. A fixed studio limits aesthetic to one set of windows and walls. Rotation produces variety, and the discipline of running the same methodology in different rooms is what makes the methodology actually portable.

### POST 40 · The product is positioning
> If the only thing SNIPED Media made were photos, this would be a service business that scales linearly with my time and caps at the number of hours in a week.

> What it actually makes: positioning. For me, for the founders I shoot, for the institutions I document. The photos are the artifact through which the positioning gets transferred.

> Different category. Different math.
