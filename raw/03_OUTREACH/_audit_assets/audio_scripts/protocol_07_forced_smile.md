# Protocol 07 · Forced Smile

**The read:** performative grin, smile reaches the mouth but doesn't reach the eyes (no Duchenne markers), expression reads "instructed to smile."

**Most common in:** young founders (Forbes 30 Under 30 type), product managers, anyone in a "show approachability" company culture. Justin Fiaschetti's photo. Most YC W26 founders.

---

## Observation · ~80 sec

```
Protocol 07 · Forced Smile. The smile in your photo is performing,
not happening.

There's a real and measurable difference between a smile that
the eye reads as genuine and one that reads as instructed. The
technical name is Duchenne markers · the muscles around the
eyes engaging when a person actually smiles versus only the
mouth moving when they're posing.

In your photo, the mouth is doing the work. The eyes aren't
following. The smile reads as a smile, technically, but the
brain doesn't register warmth. It registers performance.

This is the most common protocol at the tech founder and young
VP tier. The advice everyone gets when their photo is being
taken is "smile, look approachable, look friendly." So the
operator gives the photographer a smile. The smile lands on
the mouth, doesn't get to the eyes, and the photo reads as
caught performing.

It's not a personality issue. It's a photography direction
issue. Most photographers don't know how to trigger a Duchenne
smile, so they shoot the performed one and ship it.

The photo reads as approachable in a casual register. It reads
as undersold in an operator register.
```

## Implication · ~70 sec

```
Where this hits.

For young operators · 30 Under 30 tier, post-YC founders, recent
promotions to VP · the forced smile is the dominant photo
register on LinkedIn. Everyone has it. So it doesn't differentiate.

Worse, it lands as "engineer at his desk who happens to have
a VP title." The smile reads as nice-guy energy. The role asks
for operator energy. Gap.

On press cycle · forced-smile photos get used for soft features
and feel-good profiles. They don't get used for serious operator
coverage. Press photographers and editors filter on this without
knowing they're doing it.

On hiring · senior candidates see the forced smile and read
"this person is still in middle management posture." The photo
undercuts the title. Even if the operator is genuinely warm
and approachable in person, the performed smile reads as
unconvincing on the page.

For founders going through a viral launch cycle · this is the
critical gap. The launch makes the brand visible. The photo
makes the operator behind it visible. Forced smile during a
press cycle reads as a founder who got lucky, not a founder
who built something.
```

## The Fix · ~65 sec

```
The fix is not bigger smile or smaller smile · it's about
sequence.

You can't direct a Duchenne smile by asking for it. You direct
it by triggering it. Standard approach: get the operator
talking about something they actually care about, mid-sentence,
not at the start of a smile beat. The smile that happens during
real conversation engages the eyes. That's the frame.

On a shoot, I'll ask about the company's hardest technical
problem, or about something specific in their work. The
operator forgets the camera for two seconds, gets animated,
and that's when I shoot. The smile lands. Eyes engaged. Real.

Or · we shoot the no-smile variant. Contained, considered, jaw
engaged (Protocol 05), direct gaze. That register reads as
operator with gravity. Many operators photograph stronger in
the no-smile variant than in any smile variant.

Either way, the photo that lands isn't the photographer asking
for a smile and the operator producing one. It's a different
mechanic.
```
