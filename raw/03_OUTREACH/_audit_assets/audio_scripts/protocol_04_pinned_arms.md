# Protocol 04 · Pinned Arms

**The read:** arms stuck to the torso, no breath room between arms and body, elbows tucked tight. The frame looks compressed.

**Most common in:** tall operators trying to look smaller, or anyone who's been told to "stand still" in a way that translates to body lock.

---

## Observation · ~75 sec

```
Protocol 04 · Pinned Arms. Your arms are stuck to your torso.

The visual cue is the silhouette. When the arms are pinned to
the sides, the outline of the body becomes a solid block ·
shoulders to hips, one shape. No light coming through. No
breath room.

The eye reads breath room as confidence. When the arms have a
gap between them and the torso, the body looks open. Air around
the elbows tells the brain the person isn't trying to take up
less space.

When the arms are pinned, the photo reads as compressed. As if
the person is trying to fit into the frame rather than command
it. Especially visible on tall operators · the height should
work for them. Pinned arms make tall operators look apologetic.

This is a posture protocol that most photographers don't catch
because the person isn't slouching, isn't crooked, isn't doing
anything visibly wrong. They're just compressed. And the photo
reads compressed even when the person isn't.
```

## Implication · ~65 sec

```
This shows up on body shots more than headshots. Most VP
LinkedIn photos are tight crops · the arms are below the frame
line. But when the photo opens up to a half-body or full-body ·
hiring page hero shots, company about pages, conference speaker
imagery · the pinned arms become visible.

The exec who reads as commanding in a tight crop but apologetic
in a wider crop is running pinned arms. The protocol shows up
when the frame opens.

This also matters in candid surfaces · podcast appearances,
event coverage, behind-the-scenes shots that get reused. Body
language stays consistent from the photoshoot. If the operator
defaulted to pinned arms in the controlled shoot, they'll default
to it in the candid too.
```

## The Fix · ~60 sec

```
The fix is breath room. Literally · move the elbows out from
the body just a few inches. Hands stay where they were. Just
unhook the elbows from the torso.

On a shoot, I direct this with one cue: "show me a little air
under your arms." Operators get it immediately. The shift is
small, maybe four or five inches per arm. The visual change
is dramatic.

For a hand-on-shoulder pose · the elbow points slightly forward
and out. For hands-in-pockets · the elbows aren't glued to the
ribs. For arms at sides · slight bend, weight slightly forward.

The body opens up. The silhouette gets readable. The photo stops
looking compressed and starts looking composed.
```
