# Protocol 01 · Claw Hands

**The read:** hands clutching or gripping something visible · phone, lapel, water bottle, watch, hair. Fingers tense, not at rest. The hands say "I don't know what to do with these."

**Most common in:** operators photographed casually (event shoots, conference candids re-used as LinkedIn photos). Less common at the VP/Director tier because most VP photos are shot with hands hidden, but when it shows up it's loud.

---

## Observation · ~75 sec

```
So Protocol 01 · Claw Hands. The thing I'm looking at is what
your hands are doing in the frame.

Hands are the second thing people register after the face.
Most people don't know that. They walk into a shoot thinking
about smile, jawline, posture, maybe wardrobe. Nobody thinks
about hands. So hands end up being whatever's nearest · a
phone, a coffee, a lapel, a watch, a chair arm.

What's happening on your photo is your hands are gripping
something. They're not at rest, they're not considered, they're
not part of the composition. They're holding a thing. And the
brain reads grip as tension. Tense hands telegraph an uncertain
operator.

This is the kind of thing that's invisible until you see it,
and then you can't unsee it. Once you notice the hands, every
photo where the hands aren't considered reads slightly off.
```

## Implication · ~75 sec

```
Where this matters specifically.

For a VP tier, the surfaces where your photo gets deployed are
hiring pages, press cycles, podcast or speaker thumbnails, and
board-facing decks. On every one of those surfaces the photo
gets cropped tight. The hands are usually still in frame. And
when somebody's making a quick judgment about whether you read
as an operator, the hands do disproportionate work.

A senior eng candidate looking at your LinkedIn profile before
they apply · they're not analyzing your career. They're getting
a vibe. Tense hands knock the vibe.

A press article using your photo · same thing. The reader
spends two seconds on the image before reading the headline.
Hands shape the read.

This is why every editorial portrait of an operator you've ever
seen has the hands either hidden, on the chin, on the shoulder,
in a pocket, or just out of frame. Never gripping.
```

## The Fix · ~60 sec

```
What the fix looks like.

Three options work. One · hands out of frame, tight crop from
shoulders up. Direct, contained, no decision needed about what
the hands are doing. Two · one hand resting on the opposite
shoulder, considered, soft. Status marker placement. Three ·
hands in pockets, lapels of a jacket, or holding glasses lightly.
The keyword is lightly. Not gripped, not displayed. Held.

The same demographic shot with the same wardrobe, with the
hands in any of those three positions, reads as a different
operator. Same person. Different register. The hands are doing
that.

On a SNIPED shoot, this is one of the first things I direct.
Before the smile, before the angle. Hands first. Once the hands
are right, everything else lands.
```

## Recording notes

- Pace this slower than the others · hands are physical, easy to describe with specifics
- If you want to add a personal texture line: "I shot myself for years with my hands gripping a camera in the photo. Same protocol. Took a while to see it."
- Total audio target: ~3:30 to 4:00 when read at conversational pace

## What this script is NOT

- A pose tutorial. Not telling them where to put their hands. Telling them what their hands ARE doing and what it costs.
- A pitch. The outro covers the offer. This script is the diagnostic.
