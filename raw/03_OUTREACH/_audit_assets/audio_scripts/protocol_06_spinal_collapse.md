# Protocol 06 · Spinal Collapse

**The read:** slumped posture, shortened spine, head sitting forward of the shoulders. The photo reads "tired" even when the person isn't.

**Most common in:** desk-bound operators (engineers, finance) shot in their working environment. Years of laptop posture baked in.

---

## Observation · ~75 sec

```
Protocol 06 · Spinal Collapse. Your spine is shortened in the
photo. The head's sitting forward of the shoulders, the chest
is compressed, and the overall height of the figure in the
frame is less than it should be.

This is one of the most common protocols at the operator tier
because most operators work at laptops eight to twelve hours
a day. The posture trains. The photo catches the trained
position.

It shows up in three ways. Head forward of the spine line.
Shoulders rolled inward. Chest compressed and slightly caved.
The combination makes the body look smaller than it is, and
the head look heavier than it is.

The brain reads spinal collapse as fatigue or as defeat. Even
when the operator is sharp and rested, the photo reads tired
because the spine is telegraphing it.

The fix is mechanical, but the cause is daily life. Which means
the photo's catching a real position the operator's body
defaults to. That's why this one's stubborn.
```

## Implication · ~65 sec

```
The collapsed spine shows up most on the company about page
and on event photography. Anything full-body or three-quarter.
The collapse is visible.

For VP tier specifically, the company about page is critical.
That's where investors, candidates, press, partners go to read
the leadership team. A collapsed-spine photo next to other
operators with neutral spines makes the operator look like the
junior leader · regardless of role or title. The photo undercuts
the bio.

On hiring · same dynamic. Senior eng candidates clicking through
to read about the engineering org will visually rank the team.
Spinal collapse gets ranked down even if the operator is the
most senior person on the page.

This one also undermines the operator on speaker thumbnails
and conference promo. Tall posture reads as command. Collapsed
posture reads as someone who shouldn't be the headliner.
```

## The Fix · ~70 sec

```
The fix takes deliberate direction · this isn't one the operator
fixes themselves by trying. Telling someone to stand up straight
makes the spine rigid, which fixes one problem and creates
another · Protocol 02 · Locked Shoulders.

The correct fix is lifting from the chest, not the shoulders.
Imagine a string attached to the breastbone pulling slightly
up and forward. The chest opens, the spine lengthens, the head
naturally sits back over the shoulders. The shoulders don't
move.

On a shoot, I direct this with the cue "lift through the chest,
not through the shoulders." Two beats of practice and the body
gets it.

The change in the photo is immediate. The figure looks taller,
even though the operator hasn't grown. The head sits balanced.
The collapse is gone. The body language switches from "tired
operator at the end of a long day" to "operator who runs things."

Same person. Same wardrobe. Same setting. Different spine.
That's most of the photo right there.
```
