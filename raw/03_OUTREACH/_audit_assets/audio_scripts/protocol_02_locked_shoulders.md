# Protocol 02 · Locked Shoulders

**The read:** shoulders pulled up toward the ears, tension visible in the trapezius, no slope from neck to shoulder. The body is bracing.

**Most common in:** operators who've been told to "stand up straight" and overcorrected into rigidity. Engineering and finance VPs in particular tend to lock when a camera is in their face. Defense-tier operators (Justin Fiaschetti profile) often have this too · disciplined posture training that reads as defensive.

---

## Observation · ~75 sec

```
Protocol 02 · Locked Shoulders. This one's about the line from
your neck to your shoulder.

Most people in front of a camera lift their shoulders without
knowing it. It's a stress response. Body's protecting the neck
and head. Tiny lift, half an inch maybe, and the entire frame
changes.

When the shoulders are up, you lose the natural slope. Your
neck looks shorter. Your jaw gets crowded. Your shirt collar
sits weird. And the whole photo reads as physically braced ·
which the brain interprets as defensive, uncertain, not at ease.

This is a registration problem, not a posture problem. The
operator isn't slouching. They're locked. And locked reads as
caught off guard, even when the person is sitting calm.

The fix isn't standing taller. The fix is dropping the shoulders.
Two different physical actions, opposite outcomes.
```

## Implication · ~70 sec

```
Where it lands.

For VP and Director tier, the photo's job on LinkedIn and on
the company website is to read as someone who runs things.
Calm, in control, doesn't need to be there. Locked shoulders
read as the opposite. The photo of a person who's still figuring
out whether they should be in the frame.

This shows up specifically in talking head video too. The
moment you're recorded for a podcast or a press appearance,
the same shoulder lock is even more visible because there's
motion to read against.

On hiring · candidates make a snap call about whether the
operator at the top is steady or chaotic. The shoulders are a
tell. A photo with dropped, easy shoulders signals steady. A
photo with locked shoulders signals the opposite. Most operators
have never been told this.
```

## The Fix · ~60 sec

```
The fix is one direction · drop. Roll the shoulders back and
down. Not down and forward, which is slouching. Back and down,
which is opening the chest.

The trick I use on every shoot: have the person take a deep
inhale, then exhale all the way out, and let the shoulders fall
where they land. That's the natural position. The brain
interprets that position as steady.

Then you angle the body slightly off-camera. Not square. About
fifteen to twenty degrees. The neck-to-shoulder line becomes
asymmetric, which the camera reads as composed instead of stiff.

Same demographic, same wardrobe, same face. Shoulders dropped
and angled · different register. That's the protocol fix.
```
