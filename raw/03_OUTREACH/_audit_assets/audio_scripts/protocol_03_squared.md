# Protocol 03 · Squared

**The read:** body facing camera flat, both shoulders parallel to the lens, no asymmetry. The photo looks like an ID badge.

**Most common in:** corporate-headshot reuse cases. Generic professional shoots from 5+ years ago that the operator never replaced. Also common with photographers who default to "stand here, smile" without direction.

---

## Observation · ~75 sec

```
Protocol 03 · Squared. Body facing the camera flat. Both
shoulders parallel to the lens. No asymmetry, no angle.

This is the most common protocol I see and the one most operators
don't realize is happening. The body just defaults to square
when a photographer says "look at the camera." Because that's
what looking at the camera means physically · turn your full
torso toward it.

But the camera doesn't actually want square. The eye reads
square as flat. Square photos look like ID badges, passport
photos, security badges. The body is informational, not
authored.

For operator photos, the body has to do work. It has to suggest
movement, intention, asymmetry. Square photos don't suggest
anything. They state.

Look at any editorial portrait of an executive in the last
twenty years. Almost none of them are square. The body's always
turned a few degrees. The shoulders aren't parallel to the lens.
The asymmetry is what makes the eye stay.
```

## Implication · ~70 sec

```
Where this hits.

Square photos are everywhere on LinkedIn at the VP level. Most
LinkedIn profile photos are corporate headshots from previous
roles or photo days the company organized. The photographer
shoots one square frame per person, twenty people in an hour,
and that frame ends up everywhere for the next five years.

The problem · the role changed but the photo didn't. The body
language for senior eng IC and senior eng VP shouldn't be the
same. But if both photos are square, they read the same.

This matters most on the company about page and the careers
page. A square VP photo next to square photos of the rest of
the team reads as interchangeable. The visual hierarchy that
should exist between IC and senior leadership · invisible. The
photo isn't doing its job.

On press cycle · same thing. A square headshot in a press
release reads as a stock asset. The reporter's eye doesn't stay.
```

## The Fix · ~55 sec

```
The fix is angle. Specifically, body turned fifteen to thirty
degrees off the camera, with the head turned back toward the
lens to bring the eyes forward.

This creates asymmetry without breaking the connection. The
shoulder closest to the camera reads as the dominant line. The
neck has a natural slope. The body has direction.

Same person, same lighting, same wardrobe · the angle does the
work. The photo stops being an ID badge and starts being a
portrait.

I direct this on every shoot. The first thing after the hands
and shoulders are right is the angle. Square's the default, so
I deliberately break it. Once you see how much it changes the
photo, you can't go back.
```
