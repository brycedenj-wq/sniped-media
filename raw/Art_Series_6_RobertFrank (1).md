# Art Series 6: Robert Frank

*Trolley, New Orleans, 1955. Recreation production plan.*

---

Note up front: the Frank study doc proposed Bankers (London, 1951) as the easier port. The Art Series master doc has Trolley as Frame 6. Going with Trolley as planned. Heads up that it's the harder one. Five subjects, location dependency, and the original carries weight you can't pretend isn't there.

---

## 1. The original frame

*Trolley, New Orleans, 1955.* From *The Americans*. Frank shot the side of a streetcar in passing. Five passengers visible through five rectangular windows, top to bottom: white man, white woman, two Black children, Black woman in the back. The seating itself tells you about segregation in 1955 Louisiana. That's the politics of the frame whether you want it or not.

What's actually doing the work:

**Lighting.** Full daylight, available only. No fill. Faces are lit by whatever bounces through the open windows. Some windows brighter than others depending on what's catching reflection. The streetcar siding above and below the windows is hot, the interiors fall to mid-tone and shadow.

**Lens and camera.** 35mm Leica. Probably 50mm or close. Tri-X pushed. Loud grain. He's standing on the street, frame parallel to the side of the car.

**Composition.** Horizontal frame split into five vertical compartments by the window mullions. The mullions are the spine. Light siding above, light siding below, dark window interiors between. Slight tilt, like always.

**Pose.** Nobody is posing. Five separate emotional states. The white man is mid-blink. The boy stares directly at the camera and you can't read what he feels. The Black woman in the back looks down. None of them know they're being photographed. The frame's whole signature is five micro-narratives suspended in the in-between.

**Color palette.** B&W, near-binary. The face mid-tones are the only place the eye gets to rest.

**Wardrobe.** No wardrobe. They wore what they wore. Dark coats, hats, the period.

The signature move: five separate states framed by the same architecture. The architecture is the point. The faces are evidence.

---

## 2. Your version

Three real problems to handle before the production:

First, the original's meaning is segregation. You can't ignore it and you also can't directly restage it. Your version needs a different structural divide so the form does the work without you borrowing somebody else's politics.

Second, your aesthetic is color. The Frank study was clear: don't ape the grain, blur, B&W surface. The recreation has to commit to color while keeping the five-windows-five-states structure.

Third, you don't catch in-between. You direct. Frank's frame works because nobody knew. You'll be directing five people who know they're being shot. That's the actual test of the recreation and the whole reason it's in Phase 3.

### What stays faithful

- Five-window horizontal grid. Mullions as the compositional spine.
- Each subject in a different emotional state. Not five poses. Five states.
- Available light through the windows, with one kicker outside if the interiors get too dark (and they will).
- The slight tilt. One of the few places to actually borrow Frank's frame-as-found feeling.

### What bends to your aesthetic

- Color over B&W. Lock the vehicle exterior to one tonal family (cream, beige, putty, oxidized green, whatever you can find or paint). Lock the wardrobe inside the windows to a second family. The window frames become the tonal divider. SNIPED color blocking applied to a Frank structure.
- Wardrobe single family across all five subjects, not five different looks. Also handles the politics problem: if everyone's in the same tonal world, the segregation reading doesn't surface accidentally. The five-states reading does.
- Retouch clean. Not grainy pastiche. Color-corrected, pore-detail preserved, your usual finish on a documentary-style structure. The move nobody else is making in this lane and the actual experiment of the frame.
- Cast for silhouette, not just bodies. Five distinct head shapes through the windows is what makes the grid read as five lives, not five models.

The audit question after the shoot: did the SNIPED finish carry the five micro-narratives, or did the polish kill the in-between feeling? That's what tells you whether the lesson took.

## 3. The production

**Subjects.** Five people. Mix of models and non-models if possible. Two models and three non-models is better than five models. Non-models give you the in-between for free because they don't know what to do with their face. Mix ages and silhouettes.

**Location.** Binary fork.

*Option A, real vehicle.* Streetcar, vintage bus, heritage trolley, train car, subway car, ferry. Anything with five-plus side windows in a row. Vintage school bus rentals run $300-600/day in most US markets. Heritage trolleys at a transit museum can sometimes rent for a half-day for a few hundred more. This is a phone-call problem, not a budget problem.

*Option B, build the set.* Five window frames built into one horizontal panel, backdrop behind, lit from above. Fully inside your wheelhouse but it's a lot of build for one frame. Fallback if Option A is dead by end of day 2.

Push back on yourself before going further: don't try to do both. Pick by day 2 and commit.

**Wardrobe.** Five looks, one tonal family, decided before casting so it's shopped not styled day-of. Coats and hats are useful (period-adjacent, hide hair, give the windows shape). No logos, no flash colors, no patterns that read busy through glass.

**Lighting.**

- Real vehicle: shoot golden hour or overcast midday. Available light plus one kicker outside the vehicle to keep faces from melting into the dark interior. Battery strobe or continuous LED panel. This is your rim-light fix from the aesthetic statement, applied to a documentary frame. Don't skip it. Without it the windows go dead.
- Built set: top-down soft source mimicking sky-through-windows. Slight underexposure on faces so it reads like a vehicle interior and not a portrait setup.

**Gear.** 50mm or 35mm prime, full-frame, whatever you shoot SNIPED on. Tripod, locked. Window grids do not tolerate handheld drift. One kicker. Light meter or aggressive chimping because mixed window light will fool the camera meter every shot.

What you don't bring: four lights, smoke, a second camera body, three wardrobe options per subject. The whole point of this recreation is restraint of direction. Arrive with the SNIPED kit truck and you'll shoot the SNIPED frame.

## 4. The post

Carousel, not side-by-side. Side-by-side flattens the recreation into a comparison and your own frame disappears underneath the original.

- Slide 1: your version, full frame, horizontal. No text on image.
- Slide 2: the original, full frame. Credit text on the image bottom corner: "Robert Frank, Trolley, New Orleans, 1955."
- Slide 3: detail crop from your frame. One window. The face that came out best.
- Slide 4: behind-the-scenes or wider scene shot if you have one.
- Slide 5: text slide with what you learned.

### Caption template

> Frame 6 of the Art Series. Robert Frank, *Trolley, New Orleans, 1955*.
>
> Frank caught five strangers on a New Orleans streetcar in 1955. Five separate emotional states framed by the same architecture. Nobody posing. Nobody knowing. The in-between, five times in one frame.
>
> Recreating this meant directing what he refused to direct. Five subjects, all aware of the camera, all asked to live in the in-between anyway. Anti-everything my work is built on, which is the point.
>
> What I learned: [one sentence, written after the shoot]
>
> Original: Robert Frank, *Trolley, New Orleans*, 1955. From *The Americans*.

Don't write the "what I learned" line in advance. That sentence has to come from the actual shoot or it reads like marketing. The audit lives in the doing.

## 5. Timeline

Two weeks is realistic if Option B is on the table from day 1. If locked to a real vehicle, two weeks is the floor and three is honest.

| Days | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1-2 | Location calls. Every streetcar, bus, heritage rental, transit museum, film vehicle rental within 90 minutes. Get a price and a date. If nothing lands by end of day 2, kill Option A and order build materials. |
| 3-4 | Casting and wardrobe. Five subjects. Wardrobe family decided, looks pulled or shopped. |
| 5 | Scout or build. Real vehicle: walk through the windows, find your shooting position outside, check sun angle for the planned time. Built set: build it. |
| 6-7 | Buffer. Something will go wrong. Reshoot a wardrobe miss, replace a flake subject, fix the build. |
| 8 | Shoot. Half day, not full. Fatigue kills the in-between. Bring the kicker, leave the lighting cart. |
| 9-11 | Edit. Resist the SNIPED reflex of finishing the frame to a polish. Keep the slight tilt. Light retouch only. The audit afterward tells you whether the finish killed the moment. |
| 12 | Post the carousel. Caption written after the edit. |
| 13-14 | Reshoot buffer. You'll know by day 10 whether the keepers are actually keepers. |

One honest call: if you compress this into one week, drop Option A. Real vehicle on a 5-day timeline is a coin flip and the coin usually lands wrong. The build is duller but it ships.
