# Frame 8. Graciela Iturbide. Nuestra Señora de las Iguanas, Juchitán, 1979.

## 1. The original frame

Tight head-and-shoulders crop of a Zapotec market vendor with five or six live iguanas balanced on her head, fanning outward like a crown.

What you have to match:

**Composition.** Triangle. Chin is the lowest point, iguanas fan upward and outward. Vertical orientation. Subject centered, no rule-of-thirds offset. The frame reads as shape before it reads as person.

**Lighting.** Found, overhead. Open sky, slightly directional from above so it catches scale texture on the iguanas without crushing the face. Could be open shade with a hot patch leaking in, could be thin overcast at midday. Audit scored it 8. Not a lighting frame. Don't make it one.

**Lens.** Normal compression. 50 or 85mm. Not wide, would distort the head shape. Not super-tele, would flatten the triangle into a pancake. Working distance is close enough that you can see pore detail and scale texture both. Best guess on her file: medium format normal, handheld, close in.

**Pose.** Chin lifted. Gaze off-camera. Mouth closed. Shoulders square. Stillness, not stiffness. The energy is "I have been wearing live iguanas for fifteen minutes, hurry up." It's not posed. It's endured.

**Color palette.** B&W, full tonal range. Iguanas pull the brightest values. Skin holds mid-tones. Hair, dress, and background drop to near-black. In color this frame is noise. In B&W it's geometry.

**Wardrobe.** Dark, traditional, neckline visible but not loud. Reads deep grey to black on the file. No shine, no logo, nothing modern.

**The thing carrying the frame.** Live animals on a human head. That's the collision. Nothing else is doing the heavy lifting. Light isn't dramatic, composition is symmetrical and tight, the edit isn't aggressive. The frame works because of the impossibility of what's happening, held still.

## 2. Your version

This is the Phase 4 frame. The whole point is to leave color blocking behind for one shoot. Don't drag SNIPED into it. The study doc was direct: no B&W as default, no animals as stylistic borrow. This frame is the sealed exception. Treat it as the exercise it is, not a grid post.

What stays faithful, no negotiation:

- B&W. Full tonal range. Don't shoot color and convert with a contrast curve. Set the camera to monochrome preview, shoot raw, commit to the B&W in post.
- Tight head-and-shoulders crop. Vertical. Triangle composition with the off-element crowning the head.
- Natural light, overhead. No strobes. No softbox. If the day is overcast, that's the day.
- Chin lifted, eyes off-camera, mouth closed, shoulders square.
- One disruptive live element on the head. The collision is the frame.

What bends toward you:

- The Direction Stack architecture still runs underneath. Chin forward and slightly down to set the jawline before she lifts. Don't skip it just because the final pose is chin-up.
- Retouch dial pulled back hard. About 40% of normal. Keep skin texture. Keep grain. Don't equalize to commercial smooth. The Iturbide print has weight. Yours can too if you stop finishing.
- Don't try to cast a Zapotec market vendor. The frame fails the second it reads as costume. Cast for presence.
- The off-element doesn't have to be iguanas. See production.

The bend is small on purpose. This is the Iturbide frame, not the SNIPED-does-Iturbide frame. The takeaway is borrowing collision as a working principle. The recreation is the rep.

## 3. The production

Real talk on the iguanas first. Three options.

**Option A. Real iguanas, rented.** LA has reptile wranglers who rent green iguanas for film and photo work. Budget $300-600 for a half-day with a handler. Two or three lizards is enough. They will not stay still. The handler will tell you that. Plan for 30-40 minutes of attempts, maybe 8 readable frames where the lizards are positioned. Hits the spirit. Worth the cost if the recreation is worth doing at all.

**Option B. Taxidermy or resin iguanas.** Don't. Reads as prop in the first second. The collision doesn't work if the off-element isn't actually disruptive. Skip.

**Option C. Pivot the off-element.** If the rental is dead, pick a different live disrupting element and recreate the spirit. Pigeons on the head (real category in LA, real handlers). A live snake. The point is the collision, not the iguana specifically. If you go this route, name it in the caption. Don't pretend you recreated the iguana frame.

Pick A if the budget's there. C if not. Don't pick B.

**Subject.** One woman. Cast for presence. She has to hold stillness with something alive on her head. That's a casting filter. Ask the reptile question on the first call. Half the city of LA is not okay with it. Don't surprise her on the day.

Pay her properly. $500 minimum for a 2-hour session, plus prints. This is uncomfortable work.

**Location.** Outdoors. Backyard, side alley with a wall, rooftop. Open sky overhead, darker neutral wall behind the head. You don't want a studio. Found light is the whole point.

**Wardrobe.** Dark, simple, neckline visible. Black or deep brown. Linen or cotton with some texture. No logos, no shine, no statement jewelry. The animal is the statement.

**Lighting.** None. That's the setup. Open shade, or thin overcast, or direct sun softened by a 5-in-1 reflector held overhead by the assistant. No strobe. No softbox. The audit hit 8 on lighting because it was found. You match that by leaving it found.

**Gear.**

- Body: whatever you shoot SNIPED on, full-frame.
- Lens: 85mm, f/4 to f/5.6. Close working distance, around 4-5 feet. Manual focus, focus on the closer eye.
- Handheld is fine and probably better. The frame is not locked, you'll be recomposing as the animals shift.
- Monochrome preview on. Shoot raw. You want to see contrast structure on the back of the camera while you work.
- ISO 400-800. Grain is welcome.

**Crew.** Two. You and the animal handler. The handler is not your assistant and is non-negotiable. Don't handle the animal between shots yourself.

**Session length.** 90 minutes on location. 30 minutes setup and warm-up. 30-40 minutes of actual capture with the animal. 20 minutes wrap. The handler will tell you when the animal is done. The animal is done before you are. Plan for it.

## 4. The post

Carousel. Same architecture as the previous five Art Series posts. Don't break the pattern eight frames in.

- **Slide 1.** Recreation, full bleed, no text. Vertical hero.
- **Slide 2.** The original. Caption-credited under the image: *Graciela Iturbide. Nuestra Señora de las Iguanas. Juchitán, Mexico, 1979.*
- **Slide 3.** Text card with what you learned. Two or three lines.

If you went route C (different animal), add a fourth slide showing a detail crop of the off-element. Make the substitution legible. Don't hide it.

Caption template:

> Frame 8 of the Art Series. Graciela Iturbide, *Nuestra Señora de las Iguanas*, Juchitán, Mexico, 1979.
>
> Iturbide spent close to a decade with the Zapotec women of Juchitán. The frame everyone knows came out of the time, not the trip. A market vendor walked past her with live iguanas on her head and the picture was there because she was.
>
> Recreating this means giving up everything my work is built on. No color blocking. No studio. No locked palette. The frame has to live on shape, contrast, and one disruptive live element.
>
> What I learned: [one sentence, written after the shoot]
>
> Frame 7 was Meyerowitz. Frame 9 is Ernst Haas.

Don't pre-write the "what I learned" line. The whole point is to find out. If it flopped, say it flopped. The series is more interesting with a real loss in it than with eight clean wins. If the iguanas wouldn't stay still and the frame was a struggle, name it. If your retouch reflex pulled the file back to commercial smooth and you didn't catch it until after, name that too.

## 5. Timeline

Two weeks. The animal rental is the long-lead item.

**Days 1-2.** Phone calls. Reptile wranglers in LA. Get a quote, hold a date in week 2. Confirm the species (green iguana). If A is dead by end of day 2, pivot to C and start calling pigeon or snake handlers. Don't waste days on indecision. Pick a route and go.

**Days 3-4.** Cast the subject. Network first. Ask the reptile question on the first call. Lock by day 4. Backup on standby.

**Day 5.** Scout the location. Visit at the same hour you plan to shoot. Confirm the light, confirm the wall behind. If it's wrong, find another spot before the weekend.

**Day 6.** Buffer. Pull reference. Print the original Iturbide and tape it where you'll see it before the shoot. Confirm the B&W preview on the camera and that the raw file holds the highlights you'll need on the lizard scales.

**Day 7 or 8 (weekend).** Shoot. Morning beats afternoon. Cooler light, calmer animals. 90 minutes total.

**Days 9-10.** Edit. Cull to one frame. B&W conversion in Lightroom. Pull contrast hard but protect highlights on the scales and shadow detail in the dress. Retouch back to 40% of normal. Don't equalize the skin.

**Day 11.** Run the 8-criteria audit on the recreation. Signature and narrative weight are the gates. If signature scores under 8, the collision didn't land and the frame reads as costume. If narrative scores under 8, the subject didn't carry the stillness. Either failure is worth posting honestly. Don't reshoot to chase a clean score on a study frame.

**Day 12.** Build the carousel. Write the caption.

**Day 13 or 14.** Post.

If the wrangler dates slide a week, the whole timeline slides with them. Don't shoot a fake iguana to hit Sunday.
