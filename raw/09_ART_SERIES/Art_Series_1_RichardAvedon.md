# Art Series, Frame 1: Richard Avedon

*Production plan. Boyd Fortin, thirteen-year-old rattlesnake skinner, Sweetwater, Texas, 1979.*

---

## 1. The original frame

Avedon, *In the American West*, 1979. Boyd Fortin, 13 years old, Sweetwater Texas. Stood him against white seamless paper outside a slaughterhouse during the rattlesnake roundup. Holding a freshly skinned snake wide with both hands. Apron streaked with blood. Eyes locked into the lens.

What you're matching:

**Backdrop.** White seamless paper. Edge to edge. No floor, no horizon, no environment. The void is the whole point.

**Lighting.** Open shade. Flat, even, north-facing daylight. No key, no rim, no kicker. He killed the drama on purpose so the subject couldn't hide behind it. This is the part that breaks your default instinct, because your aesthetic statement explicitly fixes depth with rim lights. For this one, you suppress that reflex.

**Lens / camera.** He shot 8x10 Deardorff. You can't match the negative size on an EOS RP. What you can match is the *look* of it: tight sharpness across the whole subject, no shallow DOF, no falloff. Use the 85 at f/8 or f/11. Light it bright enough to keep the aperture there.

**Composition.** Centered. Vertical. Subject occupies roughly the middle two-thirds. Body cropped at the thighs. Symmetrical-ish but not stiff. The arms holding the snake form a horizontal line that breaks the verticality.

**Pose.** Standing flat. Square to the camera. Both hands gripping the snake, holding it out like he's showing it to you. Slightly slouched. Not posed. Just standing there having done it.

**Color palette in the original B&W translation.** Whites of the paper, gray flesh, dark blood streaks on the apron, the mottled snake skin. In a color recreation: pale apron, real blood (or convincing fake), skin tones, snake or whatever the equivalent prop becomes.

**Wardrobe.** Whatever the subject actually wears to do their work. The apron isn't styled. It's stained from real use. That's the rule.

---

## 2. Your version

The study doc is direct about the trap: over-direct it, lock the palette, polish the retouch, and the recreation will score 9s on composition and edit and a 5 on narrative weight. It'll look like Avedon. It won't be Avedon.

So the bend is mostly *what you don't do*.

**What stays faithful (don't fight it):**

- White seamless. Yes, it's the most copied Avedon move and the study warns about it as a borrow, but this is a recreation of a specific frame, so the void stays.
- Open shade lighting. Flat. No rim. This is the discipline you're learning here. Resisting your own fix.
- Centered, frontal, anti-flattering crop.
- Direct eye contact.
- A real subject doing real work. Not a model. Not a friend pretending. An actual person with an actual trade.
- Shoot the after, not the during. The whole frame should look like they just finished and stopped.

**What bends toward you:**

- Color, not B&W. Your signature pillar is monochromatic color blocking, not tonal range. Iturbide is where B&W gets studied later. Stay in color here.
- Clinical retouch *to your standard*, but pulled back. Don't smooth the texture out of the apron. Don't equalize every cast. Pore detail stays. Wrinkles stay. The fix is about 60% of your normal retouch pass, not 100%.
- One color decision in the wardrobe or prop that locks the palette tonally. Not styling-heavy. Just one tonal choice that makes the frame read SNIPED-adjacent without breaking the void. Example: if the subject's a butcher in a white-cream apron with deep red stains, that's already a locked palette. You don't need to add anything. If the subject's wardrobe fights the white, swap it.

**What you absolutely don't do:**

- Don't choreograph. Don't say "chin forward and down." Don't fix the hands. The whole point is the body language of someone who just did the thing, not someone being directed into a frame.
- Don't add a kicker. Don't dodge a rim in post. Let it flatten.
- Don't pick a model. The Avedon study makes this the single most important call. If the subject is a model playing a baker, the frame collapses. Find an actual baker.

## 3. The production

**Subject.** This is 80% of the shoot. Spend the time here.

You need someone with hands that show their work. Options to chase:

- Butcher (closest analog, blood-and-apron geometry already there)
- Tattoo artist (ink-stained hands, gloves, real client just walked out)
- Mechanic (grease, work coveralls)
- Welder
- Fishmonger
- Florist (less obvious, but cut stems and stained hands work)
- Baker, end of shift, flour-covered

LA has all of these. Reach the network first. If nothing pulls, walk into 3-4 shops in Boyle Heights or downtown and ask. Pay them or trade them prints. Don't make this a favor.

**Location.** The free downtown studio. Don't try to do this on location at their workplace. Avedon's whole move was deleting the workplace. The subject brings their workplace *on their body*. The setting subtracts everything else.

**Wardrobe.** Whatever they actually wear when they work. They show up in it. Don't style it. If they "cleaned up" before coming, send them back. The apron should still smell like the shop.

**Lighting setup.**

Two Godox softboxes, large as you have. Position them high and wide, both pointing flat at the subject from camera-left and camera-right at roughly 45 degrees. Same power on both. No fill behind. No rim. No hair light.

What you're imitating is north-facing open shade in a barn doorway. Big, soft, flat, even.

If the studio has a north-facing window, kill the strobes and use the window. Better. Real Avedon move.

Backdrop: 9-foot white seamless. If the studio doesn't have it, buy a roll. Tape it floor to ceiling, sweep at the bottom. Light the backdrop separately to ensure pure white, not gray-white. One Godox bare bulb or speedlight aimed at the paper does it.

**Camera and lens.** EOS RP, 85mm. f/8. ISO 200. Shutter 1/160 to sync. Tripod. Lock the framing once, do not recompose between frames. This is borrowed from Stephen Shore but it serves Avedon here too.

**Session length.** Block 3-4 hours minimum. The whole point of duration is that the first hour produces nothing useful. The subject performs being themselves. Around hour two they get tired and stop performing. That's where the frame lives.

## 4. The post

**Format.** Carousel. Not side-by-side single image (the comparison gets squashed and unreadable on mobile).

Order:

1. Your recreation, full bleed. The strongest single frame. Hero slide.
2. The original Avedon. Caption-credited on the image: "Richard Avedon. Boyd Fortin, Sweetwater, Texas. 1979."
3. (Optional) A second frame from your session. The runner-up.
4. (Optional) A behind-the-scenes detail. The subject's hands. The apron close-up. The lighting setup if it's interesting.

Don't lead with the original. Lead with yours. The original is the reference, not the headline.

**Caption template.**

```
Art Series, frame 1 of 9.

Reference: Richard Avedon, Boyd Fortin, 
thirteen-year-old rattlesnake skinner, 
Sweetwater, Texas, 1979.

Subject: [name, trade]. [Location, year].

What I was studying: [one sentence on the lesson. 
For Avedon it's: shoot the after, not the during. 
Anti-ceremony pose. Flat light as a discipline, 
not a default.]

What worked: [one specific thing.]

What didn't: [one specific thing. Be honest. The 
narrative-weight score is the test. If you over-
directed it, say so.]

Next: William Eggleston. The Red Ceiling, 1973.
```

That's the template. Keep it tight. No motivational language. No "blessed to share." The photographer credit and year are non-negotiable. The "what didn't work" line is the part that makes the series legible as a study and not just a flex.

## 5. Timeline

Two weeks is real. Tighter than that and the casting suffers.

**Days 1-3. Casting and scout.**
Reach out. Network first, walk-ins second. Goal: lock one subject by day 3. Backup subject on standby. Confirm studio access dates. Order seamless paper if needed.

**Days 4-5. Prep.**
Pull reference. Print the original Avedon and tape it in the studio so it's the first thing you see when you walk in. Test the lighting setup on yourself or an assistant the day before. Lock framing. Make sure the white reads pure on the file.

**Day 6 or 7. Shoot.**
Block 4 hours. Subject arrives in their actual work clothes. First hour: let them get bored. Don't push. Talk. Hour two and three: shoot. Don't direct into pose. Have them hold the prop they brought (or have ready) the way they actually hold it. Shoot the moment after they stop adjusting.

**Days 8-9. Select and edit.**
Pull 5-8 candidates. Edit the strongest one fully using the SNIPED v1 base preset, then pull the retouch back to about 60% of normal. Save as a session-specific preset. Process 2-3 finalists.

**Day 10. Audit.**
Run the 8-criteria prompt on the strongest frame. Compare scores against the Avedon study's baseline. The narrative-weight number is the one that matters. If it scores 5, you over-directed. If it scores 7+, the lesson took.

**Days 11-12. Final retouch.**
Lock the hero. Finalize the carousel order. Write the caption.

**Day 13 or 14. Post.**
Drop the carousel. Pin it to the grid as the first entry of the Art Series.

If a subject falls through on day 5, the whole timeline shifts a week. That's fine. The schedule serves the study, not the other way around. Don't shoot a model just to hit the deadline. The whole frame collapses if the subject is fake.
