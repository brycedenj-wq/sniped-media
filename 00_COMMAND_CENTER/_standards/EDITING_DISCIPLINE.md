# EDITING_DISCIPLINE.md

**Purpose:** The non-negotiable cut law an editor-bot obeys before any cut on the Alma Love Club deadpan-luxury swimwear film. Every rule below is mined from measured references and cites its source.

**This file is LAW. Re-read before any cut.**

The client signature is one specific thing, proven by measurement of the primary inspo (ref00, HELL/Margiela by Charlie Denis): **fast chaos cuts punctuated by long deadpan holds, in one unified warm faded-film world.** Everything here serves that. The vertical, choppy, hold-free version was rejected. Do not rebuild it.

---

## 1. SHOT LENGTH

The single most load-bearing measured fact: this is a fast film with a bimodal rhythm, not a uniform fast film.

- **Cut on a tight, fast baseline. Hold the typical shot around 1.0 to 1.1 seconds.** Set the editor's default clip length to roughly 1 second so the body reads brisk and deadpan, not languid. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: MEDIAN 1.07s, mean 1.22s, across 48 shots in 60s.)
- **Punctuate that baseline with deliberate long holds. Let about 1 in 7 shots run past 2s, up to about 4s, and reserve those holds for a beauty frame or the product/wardrobe hero.** The contrast between sub-1s chaos and 2s+ holds IS the deadpan-luxury rhythm. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: 7 of 48 shots exceed 2s, max 4.03s; 18 shots run under 1s.)
- **Mix shot lengths with high variance. Never deliver a uniform string of equal-length clips.** The spread from 0.17s minimum to 4.03s maximum (about a 24x range) is intentional dynamic range, not sloppiness. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: min 0.17s, max 4.03s.)
- **The floor below which you never cut (anti-whip):** the absolute measured minimum in the primary inspo is 0.17s, and only as a rare chaos hit, not a sustained string. Never chain whip-fast frames into a metronomic blur. A flat sub-1.5s string with zero 2s+ holds is the rejected choppy version. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: min 0.17s is the single fastest of 48; 7 shots >2s are mandatory to break it.)
- **Comparison ceilings for context.** A funniest-commercials compilation runs slower (ref01: median 1.40s, mean 1.70s, max 10.0s, 158 shots). A single-story emotion ad runs slower still (ref02: median 2.32s, max 18.5s, only 1 shot <1s). We sit FASTER than both at the baseline (ref00 median 1.07s) but borrow their long-hold discipline for the punctuation beats.
- **If the brief shifts to a single-story emotion-led ad rather than a fashion-chaos montage, slow the whole grammar:** median about 2.3s, almost no sub-1s cuts, and allow one very long establishing/payoff hold of 15s+. Match shot length to whether the piece is mood-montage or narrative. (ref02, full ad: median 2.32s, max 18.5s, only 1 shot <1s.)
- **Within an assembled action shot-sequence, cut every clip to the SAME length** so the stitched run reads as one continuous, even-tempo motion. This is the exception to high-variance: variance lives BETWEEN sequences, evenness lives INSIDE one action. (ref04, 0:46-0:53.)
- **Tighten any section that drags.** Pacing is the retention mechanism; cut down stretches that lose momentum rather than letting them run. (ref06, 6:42-6:51.)
- **Give a clip exactly as long as the line/beat stays interesting.** Hold a strong beat about 5s, cut a weaker one at about 3s. Do not equalize durations by rule; judge by the retention of the specific beat. (ref11, 71:29.)
- **Hold each reveal/pop animation to a 1 to 2 second build** (keyframe start to set value over about 1s; larger reveals over about 2s), not instant and not lingering, so beats land crisp. (ref07, 5:29-5:31.)

## 2. CUT STYLE

- **Use hard straight cuts only. No dissolves, no wipes, no transition gimmicks.** The energy comes from cut placement and shot-length contrast, not from transition effects. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: 48 clean scene-detected hard cuts, abrupt context jumps with no blended frames.)
- **Cut on motion and on a fresh tableau.** Each cut should land on a new full-body composition in a new location, not continue the same framing. Jump between unrelated deadpan tableaux to build the surreal-Americana rhythm. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: 12 distinct locations/compositions across the run, every frame a new tableau, not a coverage angle of the prior one.)
- **Anchor the fast cutting to the music's pulse.** Drive the sub-1s run as rhythmic chaos, then drop to a held shot on the musical breath. Pacing should feel scored, not metronomic. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: bimodal 18 fast / 7 held in a 60s music-led film reads as cut-to-music chaos punctuated by held beats.)
- **Match cut speed to content energy.** Fast cuts for action-packed moments; slow the cut down for emotional or dramatic parts so the viewer can feel what is being said. (ref06, 6:44-6:58.)
- **Build action moments as a shot sequence.** Capture several clips of ONE action from different angles/framings, cut them to the same length, then stitch so the action flows in continuous motion. Use it to advance the story fast AND as the opening hook. (ref04, 0:24-0:51.)
- **Build the cut from an explicit shot-to-shot chain,** each beat its own deliberate frame, not one drifting take (for example: fall to ground / rise to tree / over-shoulder / gun raises and fires / side angle). (ref09, 5:51.)
- **On a key emotional beat, do not hold on the speaker. Cut away to a reaction shot of the listener, then back.** The reaction is what loads the moment with power. (ref05, 2:04-3:08, the reaction cutaway.)
- **Order adjacent shots for meaning, not just continuity (Kuleshov).** The prior shot's emotion colors how the viewer reads the next one; choose adjacency for the read you want. (ref11, 83:16.)
- **Treat every cut as a decision about what the viewer sees AND does not see.** Shifting pauses or removing breaths changes how confident, nervous, or authentic a subject reads. Trim the A-roll to shape that perception. (ref05, 8:00-8:27.)
- **Specify the exact start and end framing of every moving shot** (the wide it starts on, the tighter box it lands on) so push-ins are authored endpoints, not a vague drift. (ref09, 10:12.)
- **A push-in/zoom is ONE shot, not two.** Do not cut from a wide still to a close still to fake a zoom; mark start and end frame on the same shot and let the move happen within it. (ref09, 9:29.)
- **A static shot with only a zoom is not a real designed beat.** A plain dynamic zoom carries no story; do not count it as a storyboarded shot or rely on it to do narrative work. (ref11, 32:31.)

**Transitions only if a ref proves one.** The only non-hard-cut moves any reference earns are motion-graphic/title transitions in an animated tutorial context (ref07), and they are NOT for our live fashion footage. If we ever use them on a title/type beat:
- **Hide the cut inside one continuous camera move** that flies/zooms from beat to beat; lay scenes out in 3D space and travel one camera through them. (ref07, 12:26-12:28.)
- **Never let a camera move come to a full stop between beats;** start the next move while the current one is ending. A dead-stop kills the premium feel. (ref07, 13:36-13:43.)
- **Peak the camera's velocity exactly on the transition point** (use the speed graph) so the eye is carried fastest through the seam. (ref07, 13:04-13:10.)
- **Shape each move quick-in / slow-out, eased on both ends;** avoid linear, even-speed moves. (ref07, 12:49-12:53 and 6:54-7:00.)
- **Tune transition pace as a deliberate value, not a preset** (make it faster, make the title pop harder); iterate the exact timing. (ref10, 7:03.)

## 3. STRUCTURE

The whole film and every sequence inside it carry one beat arc. Lock structure before any treatment.

- **Build every cut on a five-line spine before touching the timeline:** (1) situation (where we are / how the world looks), (2) desire (what the subject wants), (3) conflict (the obstacle/tension), (4) change (the turning point), (5) result (the new reality, different from the start). Lock these five before choosing transitions, grade, or music. (ref08, 2:00-2:42.)
- **Order the cut so desire lands early and conflict immediately follows.** Desire sparks curiosity and makes the audience lean in; conflict gives the reason to keep watching. A cut with no obstacle has no retention hook. (ref08, 4:42-5:15.)
- **End on a result that delivers closure:** the final beat must show the world/subject as measurably different from the opening and tie the whole piece together, not just stop. (ref08, 5:28-5:44.)
- **Structure the cut on the AIDA arc:** seize attention and pull the viewer off whatever they were doing, build interest, escalate into desire, close on a clear action/payoff. Never stall mid-arc. (ref12, 12:34-13:38.)
- **Open with the hook as the single most important beat.** Treat the first segment as the one decision point that keeps or loses the viewer; cut it for maximum stop-power. (ref11, 22:02.)
- **Every cut, regardless of genre, must have a clear beginning, middle, and end with ONE target emotion.** Decide the emotion first and let it drive the cuts. (ref06, 5:55-6:07.)
- **Architect the whole film as exactly three chapters;** give each chapter its own visual sequence paired to 2-3 songs, with consistent aesthetic framing throughout. (ref04, 4:34-4:46.)
- **Story first, tool second.** Lead with story logic before reaching for fast edits, effects, or transitions; software is only the means. (ref06, 0:42-1:12.)
- **Do not start cutting before the story's core is defined.** A messy timeline with no clear story cannot be rescued by any editing or camera trick. (ref08, 0:15-0:43, 1:31-1:43.)
- **Make the A-roll cut the foundation;** make it strong before any B-roll, graphics, or effects. The edit should flow like a stream of thought, never a checklist of clips. (ref05, 8:53-9:17.)
- **Drive structure from the spoken line / beat.** Break the line into short phrases and give each phrase its own dedicated scene/beat (one image-moment per phrase). Cut on meaning, not on a fixed clock. (ref07, 9:05-9:12.)
- **Keep every shot pulling toward the single throughline (the bowling-lane bumper).** The moment a sequence wanders into unrelated material it is very hard to recover; cut anything that lets the cut go too far afield. (ref12, 14:31-15:07.)
- **Treat the five lines as a re-entry checkpoint.** When the edit overwhelms you, stop and test every built element against one of the five lines; if it does not connect back to a line, it is a candidate to cut. (ref08, 6:07-6:19.)
- **Scale length by adding richness to the same five-beat spine, never by changing the core.** To extend runtime, expand within each beat; do not invent new structure. (ref08, 8:13-8:35.)

**Pre-cut planning law (storyboard before you generate or cut):**
- **Storyboard the entire film shot-by-shot before any clip is generated or cut,** so you can watch the whole piece start-to-end in still form and reject dead angles before they cost you. (ref09, 0:30.)
- **Keep the board as a per-shot list:** 4-6 boxed frames per page, each with a one-line description plus scene/number info; execution becomes copy-the-board, not invent-on-set. (ref09, 4:23.)
- **Annotate every frame for whether the SUBJECT moves (mark it in red).** A frame with no marked action is a static-still tell and reads as no edit happening. (ref09, 7:21.)
- **Annotate every frame separately for CAMERA movement (mark it in blue).** Never leave a shot ambiguous about whether the camera is moving; that is the difference between a live shot and a frozen one. (ref09, 8:26.)
- **Whole-watch the source at least twice before cutting** to build a mental road map, then storyboard segment-by-segment. Never start placing assets cold. (ref11, 5:21.)
- **Lock the cut order before any visual treatment:** identify the core message, conceptualize each segment, gather inspiration for the gaps, then execute, in that fixed sequence. (ref11, 10:41.)
- **The board is the floor, not a contract.** You may drop boarded frames and let a better in-the-moment angle replace one. (ref09, 11:14.)
- **Verify the finished cut by laying each boarded frame beside its matching shot and watching in sync;** divergences flag where the edit drifted. (ref09, 10:58.)
- **Match the transition gesture to story logic** when staging beats: travel the same direction to continue a thought, zoom out to widen/reveal or open a new idea. Direction carries meaning. (ref07, 11:33-11:38.)

**AI-footage handling (for generated/swapped shots):**
- **Expect identity/asset drift on every regenerate** (suit, product, character all shift slightly); plan to lock and re-feed references rather than trusting a single edited output. (ref10, 3:41.)
- **Always feed a reference image when AI-editing a specific real object/garment;** freehand prompting leaves a visible artifact (leftover logo) and will not match the real product. (ref10, 9:24.)
- **When swapping wardrobe/character/environment on real footage, hold the SAME performance and emotion through the swap.** Change the surface, keep the take's emotional read constant so the cut's rhythm survives. (ref10, 10:21.)
- **For 9:16 delivery, reframe wide source so the hero subject/product stays inside the safe frame;** a key object at the edge of a horizontal plate falls out of a vertical crop. (ref10, 2:41.)

## 4. GRADE & LOOK

One world, one grade, one accent. This is load-bearing, not decoration.

- **Grade warm, sun-bleached film stock: lifted, slightly milky blacks, gentle highlight rolloff, warm cream/tan midtones.** Do NOT crush blacks or push hard digital contrast; the look is faded analog, not punchy HDR. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: daylight desert frames read sun-faded with soft lifted shadows and warm cream/tan ground.)
- **Hold strict color discipline: one bold saturated accent (coral/red wardrobe) on an otherwise muted, desaturated ground.** Let the red be the only loud color against dusty blue sky and tan earth. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: red wardrobe is the single saturated element; the rest is restrained.)
- **Keep the grade unified across every shot: same temperature, contrast, and accent logic in daylight, night, and interior.** Night frames stay warm (neon/headlight amber), not cold-blue, so the film reads as one authored world. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: night frames carry the same warm amber bias as day frames.)
- **Build the palette as 60/30/10:** a dark variation as the main color (60%), a lighter variation as secondary (30%), one vibrant variation as accent (10%). Keep saturated color to about 10% so it reads as accent, never the field. (ref07, 1:15-1:28.)
- **Anchor the world to ONE dominant color** pulled from a colored practical in the scene, then derive everything else from it. Premium = single color identity, not a mixed palette. (ref07, 1:05.)
- **Earn the grade in-camera first:** expose brighter (more light = more information to grade), add practical lights in the background for pop, and stage saturated-color objects in frame before any color work. (ref04, 2:57-3:12.)
- **Color grade LAST, after the cut and sound are locked.** The grade sets the whole aesthetic and is the scroll-stopper; a consistent one-look grade is the finishing flavor, not the foundation. (ref04, 2:32-2:50.)
- **Establish hierarchy in every frame so the single most important element stands out,** using size, position, or contrast to steer the eye to the intended subject. (ref06, 5:11-5:21.)
- **Use color deliberately to set tone, not just to look pretty.** Hold a consistent palette and use complementary/contrasting colors to highlight key elements. (ref06, 5:21-5:32.)
- **Compose for balance.** A balanced frame reads natural; avoid cramming all elements onto one side. (ref06, 5:32-5:42.)
- **Drop background brightness to pop the subject, never lift it.** On a hero frame, sit a darkened, blurred adjustment layer between background and subject and feather it into an inverted-ellipse vignette so the background falls off. (ref07, 6:23-6:37.)
- **If the brief shifts to an emotional story register, push even softer and warmer:** heavy desaturation toward sepia/dusty-tan, low contrast, shallow depth of field on faces. Restraint in the grade carries the sentiment. (ref02, full ad: uniformly desaturated warm sepia, soft contrast, shallow-DOF faces.)
- **Treat AI 4K-upscaling as unverified for a luxury master.** Only trust a known finishing path (for example Topaz) for the final resolution pass, never a black-box upscaler. (ref10, 3:03.)

**Type/graphic discipline (only if titles are used):**
- **Use exactly two type registers:** a neutral sans for all standard text, and one special font reserved ONLY for the single word per beat you want to land. Restraint is the luxury signal. (ref07, 1:31-1:48.)
- **One emphasis per beat.** Push exactly one highlighted word forward with the accent color plus a glow; never several competing. (ref07, 5:56-6:03.)
- **Reveal text with a compound move** (position + blur + color shift + opacity together), not a plain single-property fade, which reads cheap. (ref07, 5:05-5:22.)
- **Keep shapes to squares/rectangles with moderate corner rounding (about 30-70px, scaled to the shape)** and a subtle glowing edge. Precision of the radius is the premium tell. (ref07, 1:59-2:08.)
- **Stylize element pop-ups to a stepped frame rate (posterize time to about 15fps)** for the deliberate designed stutter instead of buttery 60fps. (ref07, 7:00-7:04.)
- **For a film/camera-footage look, cut to footage with rounded-corner framing and a film-camera grade** rather than a hard full-frame clip. The rounded edge plus film look is the intended signature. (ref11, 102:58.)

## 5. PSYCHOLOGY

Written as do / do-not. Edit for what the viewer feels and wonders next, not for the next clip.

- **DO edit to translate the felt emotion of the moment.** Before each cut ask "what should the viewer feel next?" and "how is this cut motivated?", not "what shot comes next?" (ref05, 2:58-3:46.)
- **DO open a curiosity loop and delay its answer.** Every roughly 60 seconds ask what question is stuck in the viewer's head, then deliberately delay its answer to pay off later. (ref05, 4:34-5:06.)
- **DO withhold information.** Give just enough to keep them hooked and take away the rest; make the brain itch for the next frame instead of front-loading everything. (ref05, 5:11-5:23.)
- **DO apply the Zeigarnik effect.** The brain remembers unfinished things better than concluded ones; never let threads fully resolve until the very end (or not at all). (ref05, 5:25-6:26.)
- **DO answer "who am I making this for" before every editing decision** (energy level, music, sophisticated vs playful visuals) and edit from the audience's point of view, not the editor's preference. (ref06, 1:21-1:37.)
- **DO lead with desire/validation imagery before any hard "price"/sell reveal.** Show the aspirational payoff first; front-loading the cold sell scares the viewer off. Earn the want first. (ref12, 15:57-16:16.)
- **DO hold restraint and confidence in the cut.** Let the film read as the one with the least to lose; a composed, sparing cut reads as the one with all the power. (ref12, 29:28-29:40.)
- **DO put the single most powerful word of a line on screen as a large text hit synced to the moment.** Do not text-overlay neutral words, only words strong enough to land the point. (ref11, 32:42.)
- **DO show literal proof on screen for any claim** (real footage/recording), because viewers believe what they see, not what they hear. (ref11, 8:56.)
- **DO frame which proof stays prominent vs. bleeds out of frame by the target audience's interest,** not the editor's. (ref11, 72:39.)
- **DO cut the subject against the biggest authorities in the field** so the audience subconsciously ranks the subject among them; never lay generic B-roll. (ref11, 67:34.)
- **DO support a claim with a verbatim third-party quote-clip** (the actual clip, not a paraphrase) so an outside authority carries the narrative. (ref11, 56:42.)
- **DO cull only the smiling/alive faces** when showing a community/audience, and choose frames for the feeling you want, not a flat boring screen-record. (ref11, 91:51.)
- **DO reinforce the main quote with capped secondary supporting clips placed around it;** layer support, do not overdo it. (ref11, 96:33.)
- **DO match every conceptual/proof choice to the actual business and the specific audience;** every choice must have a why. (ref11, 53:56.)
- **DO color-code beats by behavior while planning** so different segment-types stay visibly distinct in the final cut. (ref11, 28:55.)
- **DO use a drawn-on underline (trim-path reveal), not a static line,** to guide the eye to a key word as it animates. (ref07, 7:48-7:55.)
- **DO keep secondary elements faded and feathered at low opacity** with only the hero at full strength, so attention stays on the hero and the frame breathes. (ref07, 20:54-21:01.)

## 6. SOUND

Music leads the cut. Visuals are only half the picture.

- **Pick and lock the music BEFORE editing (ideally before shooting).** Music dictates rhythm, pace, and emotion; cut visuals to the track's tempo, do not score after. (ref04, 1:31-2:06.)
- **Choose tracks by BPM and find the natural dips/drops; place scene changes ON those dips** so cuts land with the music instead of slicing the track manually. (ref04, 2:06-2:25.)
- **Avoid lo-fi as the bed; it reads stale and overused.** Use jazz, house, or cinematic tracks that carry narrative. (ref04, 1:43-1:54.)
- **Give every sequence a sound-design pass with named SFX layered in, not just music.** A purely visual cut feels like it is missing something. (ref04, 1:00-1:18.)
- **Treat audio as the hidden hero.** Three layers each have a job: dialogue must be clear, SFX must subtly emphasize on-screen motion, music sets the mood. Bad audio makes viewers click away over stunning visuals. (ref06, 6:14-6:38.)
- **Keep music under the voice so spoken words stay audible;** never let the bed overpower dialogue. (ref06, 6:38-6:44.)
- **On a confusion/overwhelm or accent beat, drop the music to silence to punctuate it.** Engineer the empty bar to land on the exact line, then re-enter. (ref11, 78:19.)
- **Land a music drop with an ending-drum hit only when the track is 120 BPM or higher.** Below 120 BPM the drop will not sit; check tempo before committing it. (ref11, 78:49.)
- **For a recall/callback beat, overlay earlier faces/clips at lowered opacity and carry the matching echoed audio** so the audience re-feels the prior beats while the new line plays. (ref11, 84:42.)

## 7. ANTI-PATTERNS

What NONE of the great references do. These are the rejected version. Do not rebuild them.

- **Do NOT cut everything at a uniform fast choppy length with no held beats.** A flat metronomic sub-1.5s string with zero 2s+ holds kills the deadpan-luxury signature and reads as a cheap choppy reel: exactly the vertical version the client rejected. The fast cuts only work BECAUSE 7 shots breathe past 2s. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: 7 shots >2s are mandatory.)
- **Do NOT crush blacks, add cold-blue night, or punch hard HDR contrast.** The faded-film, warm, lifted-shadow grade is load-bearing; a clean punchy digital look reads as generic and breaks the analog-Americana mood. (ref00, 0:00-1:00: every frame keeps shadows lifted and temperature warm.)
- **Do NOT ship a busy, mixed, or inconsistent grade** where shots jump between cold-green, neutral-broadcast, and warm interiors with no unifying look. A compilation-style grade destroys the authored single-world feel. (ref03, full reel: the look strip jams cold greens, lunar grey, and warm interiors with no shared grade, the opposite of HELL's unified world.)
- **Do NOT use a flat, contrasty, oversaturated digital broadcast grade with no color discipline.** Avoid the neutral "TV commercial" look where skin and product sit in clean even light with no accent-on-muted restraint. (ref01, full reel: flat neutral broadcast lighting, no signature grade or accent discipline.)
- **Do NOT default to tight talking-head coverage cut from near-single-takes.** Holds of 11s+ on a single framing are the inert anti-pace; the fashion film must keep moving between distinct full-body tableaux. (ref13, full clip: median 11.7s, near-single-take, is the anti-pattern.)
- **Do NOT over-engineer the edit chasing novelty.** The whole method is shot-sequence + sound + grade; less is more, resist adding clever complexity. (ref04, 3:36-3:46.)
- **Do NOT let AI auto-cut the footage and then pour all effort into animations/transitions/plugins.** Flashy motion graphics do not hook or move people; emotion, attention, timing, and intent do. The best editing is invisible; if a viewer says "that editing was cool," you missed the mark. (ref05, 8:39-10:08.)
- **Do NOT keep a shot just because it is beautiful.** Cut any shot that does not advance the story, even the best-looking shot you have. Story contribution is the only keep criterion. (ref06, 6:07-6:13.)
- **Do NOT improvise shots/angles on the timeline.** Made-up angles read good in the moment then turn out flat; lock the angle list in advance and execute it. (ref09, 1:48.)
- **Do NOT replace available real proof with a built animation.** If hard proof exists, cut to it first; fall back to animation only when no proof exists. (ref11, 45:53.)
- **Do NOT reuse the same visual device for consecutive similar beats.** Vary the treatment so the cut never reads as a repeated pattern even when the content rhymes. (ref11, 74:44.)
- **Do NOT re-watch your cut from the editor's perspective.** Editors over-polish the first 25-30% and judge by craft pride, hiding repetition and front-loading. Switch to the audience's perspective to catch it. (ref11, 76:16.)
- **Do NOT add 3D space / camera-push staging as decoration.** Use it only when it genuinely helps the viewer parse a crowded multi-element beat; never add it because everyone uses it. (ref11, 47:58.)
- **Do NOT chase 100% perfection on every shot.** Reserve frame-perfect finishing for the hero/showreel beats and let secondary connective shots stay good-enough. Over-perfecting every frame stalls the edit. (ref12, 23:39-24:05.)
- **Do NOT let a clip stand on the words alone, and do NOT lead with the cold sell.** Show proof, and earn desire before the reveal (cross-referenced from Sections 5 and 6). (ref11, 8:56; ref12, 15:57-16:16.)

*End of LAW. If a proposed cut violates any rule above, do not make the cut. Re-read before the next session.*
