Subject + Camera Move + Mood. "Medium close-up, eye level. Subject turns slightly. Slow dolly-in. Soft cinematic lighting." One clear camera move looks more cinematic than stacked
moves. Writers describe scenes; directors describe shots. The platform rewards directors. Law V — Shot Structure Goes at the Top Every video prompt begins with the structural
declaration: number of shots, total duration, aspect ratio. Everything else follows. This single move — declaring the shape of the output before describing its contents — is the
difference between coherent sequences and random clips. Seedance format examples in Part VII demonstrate this across every genre. Law VI — Name What the Camera Is Not Doing
Negative camera constraints keep perspective locked. "No cuts, no zoom, natural head movement" preserves first-person POV. Without it, models default to cutting between angles and
the perspective breaks. The rule generalizes — anytime you need to lock a variable, state explicitly that it must not change. This is the Higgsfield expression of the broader
Constraint Sandwich principle. Law VII — Sequential, Never Parallel The production pipeline is sequential. Pre-production (identity, keyframes, prop sheets, shot library) builds
the assets that production (animation, camera simulation, model selection) will consume. Post (review, upscale, assembly) refines what production produced. Never generate final
assets in one pass. Each step builds on the last, and skipping upstream steps compounds downstream into wasted credits and inconsistent output. Position yourself as virtual
production for solopreneurs, not AI prompting. The frame is studio-in-a-box. Sell consistency and systemization, not randomized output. Part II — The Platform Architecture

Production Pipeline Never generate final assets in one pass. The pipeline is sequential and each step builds on the last. The following is the complete flow from blank project to

Export into your editing workflow for captions, music, cuts, and final formatting. Store every asset in organized project folders. The library compounds across projects — you will
reuse it. The Consistency System Consistency is not achieved by accident. It is imposed by a specific set of prompting and reference practices that operate at every stage of the

consistent 2D anime illustration style throughout all frames. Part VII — The Prompt Architecture Higgsfield rewards structured prompts. The frameworks below are not suggestions;
they are the patterns the models were tuned to respect. Every production-grade prompt in the Higgsfield prompt library follows one of them. 7.1 The Universal Prompt Structure Every
video prompt — regardless of model — should be built in this order. Shot structure declaration: number of shots, total duration, aspect ratio. Always at the top. Reference tags:
identify every input and its role (character, style, environment). Style declaration: aesthetic, rendering approach, film quality specs (see the Film Quality Block below). Timed
segments: break duration into named time blocks (0–3s, 3–6s) with specific action per block. Camera behavior: what the camera is doing AND what it is NOT doing. Negative
constraints: "no 3D, no cartoon" for realism; "no cuts, no zoom" for locked POV. 7.2 The Shot-Level Formula Composition + Subject + Camera Move + Mood This is the director's
grammar for a single shot. Example: "Medium close-up, eye level. Subject turns slightly. Slow dolly-in. Soft cinematic lighting." One clean structure, four elements, no prose. 7.3
The Film Quality Block A reusable block of cinematography-aware vocabulary that materially improves output quality across models. Paste it as a style declaration into any cinematic
prompt. Cinematic lighting, photorealistic, 35mm film quality, professional color grading, sharp focus, film grain, depth of field mastery, ARRI ALEXA aesthetic. For ultra-realism
add: "no 3D, no cartoon, no VFX." For additional realism layering: "grounded realism, strong 35mm film look, heavy film grain, sharp but imperfect focus, noticeable focus
breathing, motion blur on fast actions, halation on highlights, soft highlight rolloff, slightly desaturated tones, practical VFX feel, minimal CGI look, natural imperfections."
7.4 The Five Seedance Format Recipes Seedance 2.0 is the workhorse for action, animation, transformation, and POV content. The platform has five battle-tested format recipes. Each
has a fixed structural formula. Learn the formula once; swap the content infinitely. Recipe 1 — Transformations The highest-performing Seedance format. A character transforms in
response to a threat, neutralizes it, and returns to normal. TRANSFORMATION FORMULA • 6 shots / 15 seconds / 16:9. • Number each shot. Describe the exact action in each. •
Escalation arc: calm → threat → transformation → combat → transformation back → calm. • End where you started. The contrast with the middle is what makes it land. • Add 'no 3D, no
cartoon, no VFX' to force ultra-realism when skin looks plasticky. • Add 'add a visual gag in the background' and Seedance will invent one for comedy. Production example — a girl
on a bus transforms into a green-armored robo-girl, destroys a rampaging creature, and drops back into her seat as a human: Montage, multi-shot action Hollywood movie, don't use
one camera angle or single cut, cinematic lighting, photorealistic, 35mm film quality, professional color grading, sharp focus, high detail texture, film grain, depth of field
mastery, ARRI ALEXA aesthetic. Shot 1: Wide shot inside the bus, passengers pressing against windows... Shot 2: Close-up on the girl as she glances left, lowers sunglasses... Shot
3: Medium shot from outside as she pulls through the window... Shot 4: Medium close-up on the roof as green energy ribbons spiral up... Shot 5: Wide low-angle as she fires a
devastating energy beam... Shot 6: Medium shot inside the bus as she drops through the window... Total: 15s / 6 shots / 16:9 Recipe 2 — Orbs Continuous first-person POV. The camera
IS the character's eyes. One shot, hyper-chaotic handheld motion, 15 seconds. What changes across Orbs is the power, the environment, and the enemy — the structure stays identical.
ORB FORMULA • 1 shot / 15 seconds / 16:9. • First-person POV — the camera is the character's eyes. • Hyper-chaotic handheld, completely unstabilized, wide-angle lens with strong
distortion. • Specify VFX inline using bracket syntax: [VFX: branching electric circuits pulsing with white-blue current]. • Three-beat action: engagement → escalation →
titanic-enemy finish. • RAMPS TO SLOW MOTION on a key beat, SNAPS BACK for impact. • End with an SFX block listing specific sounds in sequence — Seedance matches them to action.
Recipe 3 — POVs Locked first-person perspective for immersion. The single instruction most operators miss is explicitly telling Seedance what the camera is NOT doing. POV FORMULA •
1 shot / 15 seconds / 16:9. • First-person, locked perspective. • Explicit negative camera constraint: 'No cuts, no zoom, natural head movement.' • Without the negative, Seedance
defaults to cutting between angles and breaks the illusion. • Sometimes the shortest prompts hit hardest — the Horses POV is a single sentence. Recipe 4 — Fights Choreographed
combat with a clear escalation arc. The best fight prompts describe the exact choreography beat by beat — Seedance executes what you write. FIGHT FORMULA • Clear location with
atmospheric specificity. • Clear power mismatch (size, weapon, style) creates visual interest. • Defined escalation: setup → first strike → counter → climax → resolution. • Speed
ramps inline: 'RAMPS TO SLOW MOTION as the kick connects. SNAPS BACK.' • Reference: Guy Ritchie speed-ramping with Snyder impact slow-motion. • Describe physics as precisely as
actions — dust clouds, impact ripples, debris trajectories. Recipe 5 — Animation Stylized animated sequences with timed segments. Animation in Seedance works best when you break
the 15 seconds into explicit time blocks and describe each one individually. ANIMATION FORMULA • Use a keyframe image as the style reference (<<<image>>> is the first keyframe and

create a character keyframe and a recurring-prop sheet (multi-angle orthographic views). The prop appears in every single scene; getting it right first saves hours. Generate scene
keyframes in Soul Cinema first. Each scene gets its own visual style established before any animation. Edit keyframes to match your character using Nano Banana Pro. Preserve
everything else — lighting, environment, camera angle — explicitly. Animate with Seedance: keyframe + character reference + previous scene's video as context for continuity. Use
transition devices — a watch activation, a portal, a white flash — to create clean visual breaks between styles while preserving narrative continuity. The Styles That Work Across
Scenes Seven animation styles have been production-tested to work together in a single film without breaking character consistency: 2D Anime — clean flat cel-shading, thick
confident outlines, limited palette. French Graphic Novel / Moebius — flat illustration, bold outlines, muted sophisticated palette. Chibi Low-Poly — stylized 3D with exaggerated
proportions and simplified geometry. 3D Claymation — stop-motion aesthetic with deliberate frame-rate artifacts. Black & White Manga — screentone shading, speed lines, dramatic
angles. 2D Cartoon in Live Action — stylized character over photographed environment. Live-Action Cinematic — the "default" for grounding; makes the others land harder by contrast.

activation sequence — the character taps a smart-watch feature that triggers a white-out teleportation. Each scene ends with the flash; the next scene opens after it. The device
works because it converts a jarring style shift into a plot beat the viewer expects to look different. Transition writing pattern Scene N ends on: Single frame of pure white. Smash
