confrontational. Angled reads at ease. Turned reads profile and elegant. 6. Arms and hands In pockets, crossed, at sides, touching face, holding an object. Hands in pockets solve
the what-do-I-do-with-my-hands problem for men. For women, one hand at the collar or in the hair does the same. 7. Head and eyes Chin up or down. Nose toward camera or away. Eyes
to camera, past camera, or to a specific point. Chin slightly down and forward reduces double chin. Nose around toward the light. Eyes to the lens for connection, past the lens for
mood. Adjust one point, leave the other six. The pose changes. Adjust a second point, the pose changes again. Most working photographers cycle through all seven within a two-minute
pose session and generate twenty usable frames without ever asking the subject to 'do something else.' 7.2 The Male Default Most untrained male subjects default to standing square
to the camera with arms at their sides and hands crossed over the crotch. This is the worst baseline pose available. The recovery sequence: Turn the body toward the light source.
Not the torso only. The feet, the legs, the whole person. Put the subject on a turntable and rotate the turntable. Put the hands in the pockets. Thumbs inside the pocket, fingers
on the outside of the pant. Not the other way around. Thumbs outside with fingers in is the amateur mistake. Feet close together. The stance is narrower than most men think it
should be. Bend the front knee. Slightly. This is the single highest-yield adjustment in male posing. Lean the torso forward. Slight glorified slouch. Chest and shoulders toward
the camera, gut away. Chin forward and slightly down. Reduces the double chin. Thins the jaw line. If the subject complains the stance feels unnatural, it is because it is. The
natural pose is the one that photographs worst. The posed stance photographs well. This is one of the few cases where the photographer has to override the subject's discomfort
briefly to get the image. 7.3 The Female Default The mirror version of the male default. Untrained female subjects default to standing square with arms stiff at their sides. The
recovery uses the same seven points but the levers produce different results. Shift weight onto one leg. The other leg relaxes. Immediately reads more natural. Put the resting leg
on tiptoe or slightly bent. Activates the knee and produces a curve through the hip. Bring one hand to a point on the body. The waist. The collar. The hair. The one-hand anchor
solves the arm problem the way the pocket solves it for men. Angle the shoulders away from square. Rotate slightly. Chin slightly down, nose around toward the light. Dominant eye
closer to the lens. Let the eyes commit. Camera, past camera, down, or away. Eyes that wander read as uncommitted and the image loses. 7.4 The Rotation Trick The fastest way to
generate variation without changing the pose is to rotate the subject. A forty-five-degree rotation from a starting pose is a new pose. A ninety-degree rotation is another. Back to
the camera with the torso twisted toward the lens is another. One pose times four rotations is four images. Most photographers stop at one. If you find yourself out of poses,
rotate. A tiny rotation is a new pose. 7.5 Chairs, Couches, and Leans Subjects who are standing with nothing to do with their bodies are the hardest to pose. Subjects who are
sitting, leaning, or supported by a prop are the easiest. The structural reason: the prop absorbs the what-do-I-do-with-my-body problem. A couch lets the subject relax into the
furniture. Their body finds its own resting position. The photographer works with what emerges. A chair gives the subject a seat and a back to lean against. A crossed leg or an
angled torso generates pose variation without any direction. A wall to lean on does the same outdoors. One shoulder against the wall, weight on the wall-side foot, and the rest of
the body arranges itself. A ledge to sit on, a rail to rest a hand on, a staircase to sit on. Any structural element the subject can physically relate to. For difficult subjects,
start with a lean. Get the first frames when they have something to hold onto. Move to standing only after they are warmed up. 7.6 The Seven-Second Warm-Up The subject arriving on
set has never done this before today, or has not done it in a long time. The first seven seconds of posing are spent on warm-up, not on shooting. Have them shake out their legs.
Roll their shoulders. Take a breath. The stiffness is physical, not mental, and the fix is physical. Subjects who skip the warm-up pose stiff for the first ten frames and then
relax. Subjects who warm up pose relaxed from frame one. 7.7 Addressing Insecurities Up Front The single most useful pre-shoot question is: is there anything you are self-conscious
about that you want me to mind while we shoot? This is asked before the first pose, in a matter-of-fact tone, as a standard part of the intake. The question does three things. It
signals that the photographer cares about how the subject looks and feels. It surfaces the insecurity the subject was going to try to hide anyway. It gives the photographer
permission to address the insecurity directly (e.g., 'stand a bit forward so your stomach stays flat to camera') without it reading as a criticism. The risk in asking is that the
photographer has to then honor the answer. Asking and ignoring is worse than not asking at all. If the subject tells you they are self-conscious about their arms, the arms get
attention on every pose. Not once. Every pose. 7.8 Communication as Beat The rhythm of direction matters as much as the content. A flat delivery reads as uncertain: 'can you put
your arm here, can you put your finger there, can you put your leg like this.' A rhythmic delivery reads as confident: 'keep the arm up, beautiful, chin down, hold, eyes here, one
two three.' The subject reads the rhythm as competence and relaxes. Specific rules: Use body-part names, not left and right. Left and right require the subject to translate from
their perspective to yours, which takes time and kills the beat. Point when you can. 'That shoulder' with a finger pointed is faster than 'your left shoulder' in words. Confirm the
hits. 'Yes, hold that.' 'That's it, stay there.' Subjects who do not get confirmation assume they are wrong. Keep the camera off your face when you are directing. Subjects do not
read a lens the way they read a face. If they cannot see you, they cannot read you. 7.9 Family and Group Posing Groups have a different problem set. The core rule: every person in
the group must have at least one arm visible. Arms that are wrapped around other people disappear into the group and read as amputated torsos. The recovery: One hand in a pocket,
the other on a shoulder or holding a hand. Hold hands across the group. Creates visible arms and physical connection in the same move. For men in suits, hands in pockets is nearly
always the answer. Both arms wrapped around family members opens the suit jacket and ruins the silhouette. Stagger heights. Do not line everyone up at the same level. Seated,
standing, leaning produces a natural triangle composition. Part VIII: The Call Sheet The call sheet is where the six layers consolidate into one document. Everyone who is attending
the shoot reads from the same sheet. The photographer, the model, the stylist, the hair and makeup artist, and any assistants. If the call sheet is accurate, the shoot runs itself.
If the call sheet is missing or sloppy, every layer of the production stack has to be re-explained in person at call time. 8.1 What the Call Sheet Contains THE WORKING CALL SHEET
Date and call time. Not the shoot time. The time everyone arrives. Location with full address, parking notes, and a contact number for the space. Shoot duration and the rough
schedule, including breaks and wardrobe changes. Moodboard (embedded or linked). The working version everyone agreed to. Subject / model name and contact. Team list with roles and
contacts. Wardrobe brief. What the model should bring or wear. What the team is providing. Lighting plan, summarized. Natural or studio. Primary modifiers. Shot list. The images
that must be delivered by end of shoot. Ranked in priority order. Client contact (if applicable) and deliverable expectations. Weather forecast for outdoor shoots. A contingency
location. A single line that tells everyone what the shoot is about. One sentence. 8.2 Distribution The call sheet is sent the day before the shoot. Not the morning of. Not two
hours before. The day before. Everyone has a chance to raise a conflict while there is still time to adjust. To the model, with the condensed moodboard and the wardrobe notes
highlighted. To the team, with the full moodboard, the lighting plan, and the shot list. To the client, with the shot list and the deliverable schedule. A copy to yourself, printed
if you work off paper on set, on the phone if you do not. 8.3 The One-Sentence Shoot Brief The hardest and most useful line on the call sheet. One sentence that tells every reader
what this shoot is about. Not what it shows. What it is. A single sentence is enough to align everyone's intuition for the hundred small decisions that happen on the day. Examples:
'A commercial beauty campaign for a clean-ingredient skincare brand, shot editorial, not advertising.' 'A personal portrait series of working musicians in their practice spaces,
low-key natural light.' 'A men's fashion lookbook for a Fall / Winter collection, warm palette, shot on location in a 1970s-styled apartment.' If the sentence is hard to write, the
shoot is not clearly scoped and the issue is upstream of the call sheet. Fix the upstream scope before the call sheet is finalized. Part IX: Field Fundamentals The carried craft.
The things a working photographer has to know so deeply that they do not think about them on set. These underlie every other layer in the book. 9.1 Rule of Thirds for Portraits The
rule of thirds is usually taught as a compositional overlay and left there. In portraits, it is an operating system. Place the subject on the left or right third of the frame, not
the center, unless the background is untextured and you are deliberately going symmetrical. The face goes on the top third line, preferably where the top third line and the side
third line intersect. The subject faces into the frame, not out. A subject looking out of the frame pulls the viewer's eye out with them and the image loses its center. If there is
a second element of interest in the background (a building, a landmark, a tree), place it on the other third line so the frame has two anchors. For symmetrical portraits against
clean backgrounds, the rule inverts. The face goes dead center, but still on the top third line vertically. The image reads as intentional rather than as a mistake. 9.2 Background
Blur The professional look of a portrait is often a function of the background blur. The settings are simple. Aperture priority mode. The smallest f-number your lens allows. f/2.8,
f/1.8, f/1.4. The smaller the number, the shallower the depth of field. Zoom to the longer end of your lens. Longer focal length compresses the background and blurs it further.
Focus on the eyes. Specifically the near eye. Not the bridge of the nose. Not the cheek. The eyes are the sharp anchor the viewer reads. On a phone, the equivalent is Portrait
mode. On a digital camera, the four steps above produce the result. A portrait shot with a clean blurred background reads as a professional image before any other decision about
the image matters. 9.3 Exposure for the Subject The camera will meter the whole frame and average the exposure across it. For portraits, the subject's face is the only thing that
has to be exposed correctly. The background can be bright, dark, blown out, or crushed; the face has to be right. For a subject backlit by a bright sky, the camera will expose for
the sky and silhouette the subject. The fix is either to add light to the subject's face (a flash, a reflector, or a closer move with a brighter key) or to move the subject so the
light is on the face rather than behind it. Spot metering on the face is the most reliable technical fix. The camera meters only the face and exposes correctly for it regardless of
the rest of the frame. For darker skin tones, the meter will often underexpose. Add two-thirds to one full stop of exposure compensation. For lighter skin tones, subtract
two-thirds to one full stop. The meter is wrong on both ends of the range if you let it average. 9.4 The Mood of the Frame is Decided by Three Things Light. Background. Pose. Every
photograph's mood, for better or for worse, is produced by these three variables. The moodboard in Part II is trying to communicate about these three. The location scout in Part IV
is about these three. The set design in Part V, the lighting in Part VI, the posing in Part VII. All of it converges on these three variables. When a photograph is not landing and
you cannot tell why, check the three in order: Is the light doing what you want it to do? Quality, direction, ratio. Is the background working with the subject or against it? Clean
or busy. Color relationship with the subject. Is the pose serving the frame or fighting it? Does it use the light and the background correctly? Most frames that are not landing
fail on one of these. Fix the one that is failing. Do not reshoot until you have diagnosed which of the three is the problem. 9.5 The Word-of-Mouth Truth Most of the working
photography business is word-of-mouth. A subject who leaves a shoot happy with how they were treated and happy with the images they received is the single most valuable asset in
your pipeline. They will refer. They will repost. They will come back. A subject who leaves unhappy is the same machine running in reverse. Every shoot is an input to the next
shoot. The call sheet, the moodboard, the in-person experience, the delivered images. All of it is the product. The photographer who treats each shoot as a standalone event is
leaving the compounding effect on the table. You do this enough times and it becomes easy. Then you never think about it again. Appendix A: Pre-Shoot Checklist Run this the day

stands. Clean, commercial-grade soft-light portrait. C.2 Two-strobe flat-lit key plus beauty dish top Two strobes firing into a large diffusion panel positioned in front of the
subject. One beauty dish positioned above the subject angled down for top light and structural shaping. Background: white seamless or a clean matte wall. C.3 Classic key / fill /
rim Octabox key, forty-five degrees to subject, above eye level. Reflector or weaker softbox as fill on the opposite side, one stop lower. Strip softbox with grid behind and above
for rim. The ninety-percent setup for commercial portraits. C.4 Hard-light fashion Zoom reflector or bare strobe with a fifteen-degree grid as a hard key. No fill or minimal fill
from a reflector. Negative fill (poly board) on the shadow side to deepen the shadow. For editorial, fashion, and high-contrast beauty. C.5 Outdoor natural at 4:30 p.m. Subject
positioned so the low sun is behind them, providing rim light along the hair. A reflector in front held by an assistant, bouncing the same sunlight back onto the face as key. The
entire setup is one sun and one reflector. Produces the warm, cinematic portrait most clients will associate with professional work. Appendix D: Pose Starter Menu The first three

BEFORE THE SHOOT Moodboard: five pages, locked, delivered to model and team. Model: confirmed, wardrobe sent, moodboard reviewed. Location: scouted at intended time of day, shade
and clean background confirmed, backup identified. Set: era filter applied, color palette locked, elevation and depth planned, anachronisms removed. Lighting: diagram drawn,
modifiers packed, position rehearsed. Pose: starting vocabulary selected for the subject default, three variations per look. Call sheet: sent the day before. ON THE DAY Arrive
before the model. Test the light without the subject in it. When the model arrives, address insecurities in the intake. Warm up the body. Shake out the legs. Roll the shoulders.
Light, Location, Layout. In that order. Every new spot. Seven posing points. One change at a time. Confirm hits. Keep the beat. Do not hide behind the camera. Meter for the face.
Protect the face. AFTER THE SHOOT Deliver the images. On time. Without being asked. Thank the model. Send the follow-up DM. Save the location, the lighting setup, and the pose
notes to the database. Identify the one thing that worked best in the shoot. Identify the one thing that failed. Close the loop. The next shoot starts the moment this one ends.
Closing The Direction Stack handles what happens once the subject is in front of the lens. The Production Stack handles everything that decides what that moment looks like before
it arrives. Both are craft. Neither is replaceable by the other. Run the six layers every time. Not because a book said to. Because the shoots that land are the shoots where the
layers were run, and the shoots that miss are the shoots where one of them was improvised. You will get fast at it. The first full stack run-through on a shoot takes a week. The
tenth takes a day. The hundredth is reflex. By then, the stack is the job, and the job is easy. Build the library. Run the stack. Do the work.
