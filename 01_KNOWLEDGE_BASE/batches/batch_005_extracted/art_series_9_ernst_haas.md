# Art Series 9: Ernst Haas

*Bullfight, Spain, 1956. Recreation production plan.*

---

Note up front: Frame 9 is the graduation move of the series. By week 17, eight constructed-and-controlled frames are in the bag and Haas is the deliberate dismantling of that reflex. Every production decision below should be measured against one question: am I actually surrendering control, or am I building a controlled frame that imitates surrender? Arrive on set with the SNIPED kit truck and run it like a SNIPED shoot, the assignment is already failed.

---

## 1. The original frame

*Bullfight, Spain, 1956.* Kodachrome. Leica with what was almost certainly a 50mm. Shutter dragged to 1/15 or slower. The matador becomes a vertical streak of red and gold against the brown smear of the bull. You can't read who he is. You can't read the bull's expression. You can barely read the architecture of the ring. The frame is not a record of a bullfight, it's a record of what the bullfight *felt like* moving past the camera.

What's actually doing the work:

**Color palette.** Brown-dominant field (the bull, the dirt, the shadow side of the ring) carrying maybe 70 percent of the frame. Red and gold from the matador's suit and cape carrying the punctuation. Saturated-single-tone-field-with-one-punctuation. Two tones, one is the world, one is the event.

**Lighting.** Available daylight. Hard sun in the ring, deep shadow under the seating. He didn't direct the light. He shot what the day gave him. His lighting score in the audit was capped at 7 on purpose. Any added light kills the frame.

**Lens choice.** 50mm. Eye-level. Standing in the cheap seats or close to the barrera. Not a long lens. He's compressing nothing. The intimacy is positional, not optical.

**Composition.** Figure roughly on a golden-section vertical, not dead center. The bull's smear and the matador's streak create a horizontal sweep across the middle third. A lot of negative-ish brown above and below. The frame is more empty than full.

**Pose.** Anti-pose. Mid-pase, mid-twist, mid-something. There is no decisive moment. There is duration. A 1/15 exposure means the picture is the full eighth-of-a-second of motion compressed into one image, not a single instant.

**Wardrobe.** *Traje de luces.* Saturated red and gold embroidery, pink stockings, black slippers. He didn't dress the subject, the tradition did. The fact that the costume already lives in the exact tonal register Kodachrome was made for is part of why the frame works.

The signature move stacked: Haas points the camera at a thing the world is already saturating, slows the shutter until the figure dissolves into color, and lets the picture be about motion and tone instead of identity and event.

## 2. My version

Three things to handle before building the production.

First, I can't shoot a bullfight. I'm in LA and wouldn't shoot one if I could. The recreation is structural, not literal. I'm recreating the Haas *frame logic*, not the subject. Saturated-tone field, one figure dissolved into color, motion as the carrier. The bullfight is the costume the frame happened to be wearing. Strip it.

Second, this is the frame where I deliberately give up the SNIPED control stack. Locked palette stays. Direction Stack does not. Clinical retouch does not. Rim light does not. The aesthetic statement names "shoot the in-between" as the narrative-weight fix and Haas is the formal mechanism behind that fix. Keep the rim and the polish on this one and the look gets recreated while the lesson gets missed.

Third, I'm a portrait operator. The study was firm: don't borrow the no-pose method wholesale. So this isn't no direction. It's *minimal* direction aimed at producing motion, then accepting whatever happens inside the motion. Set the conditions and let the camera and the subject do the rest.

### What stays faithful

- Brown-dominant field with red-and-gold punctuation. Saturated-field-with-one-punctuation borrow, applied verbatim.
- Slow shutter, 1/15 or 1/8, on a tripod.
- Available or continuous light only. No strobe.
- A figure in motion, not a figure in pose.
- 50mm or 35mm lens, eye-level, no compression tricks.
- Cropping that lets the figure dissolve, not lock down.

### What bends toward SNIPED

- The frame is constructed, not found. Building the brown field, not stumbling into a Spanish bullring. Construction-vs-finding is the axis the study said I can't fake my way across, so don't try.
- The locked palette before anyone arrives is still the discipline. Wardrobe is one tonal family with one event color (red on brown with a gold accent). Already how I work, just pushed past usual SNIPED restraint into actual saturation.
- The retouch is pulled back hard, maybe 30 percent of the usual SNIPED edit. Same protocol as the Herzog plan. Skin still clean, color casts equalized, pore detail intact, but no rim-in-post, no edge dodge to fake separation. If the in-camera frame doesn't carry, it doesn't get saved in Lightroom.

## 3. The production

**Subject.** A dancer, not a model. Specifically someone with a flamenco, contemporary, or trained-movement background who can sweep fabric on cue and make the body do something legible mid-motion. A model will pose. A dancer will move. I need movement.

Find them through a dance studio in Mid-City, Hollywood, or Highland Park. Backstage post for "dancer for a 2 hour studio session" works as backup. Pay them or trade prints. Don't make this a favor.

The flamenco lineage is the right one because the bullfight and flamenco share the same tonal palette and the same red-fabric-as-extension-of-body language. I'm not cosplaying a matador. I'm hiring a dancer who already moves the way the frame requires.

**Wardrobe.** Deep saturated red. Tailored, not loose. A red dress with movement, or a red suit, or red pants and a red top. Doesn't matter as long as the silhouette is clean and the tonal block is unbroken. One gold accent, single piece. Gold belt, gold hairpiece, gold sleeve detail. That's the punctuation inside the punctuation, the move the study said I'm not currently making.

A red fabric panel or shawl, three to four feet, also red, slightly different shade if possible so the sweep reads as its own element instead of disappearing into the outfit. Pink slippers or red flats. No logos. No black accents. Black absorbs light and breaks the brown-red-gold trinity.

**Location.** Studio. Brown is the production problem to solve. Three options ranked by realism:

*Cheap path.* Deep ochre or russet brown seamless paper, lit unevenly so it has tonal variation top to bottom. The found-painting eye applied to a backdrop, second highest-leverage Haas borrow. Don't light it flat. Let one corner go darker. That tonal drop becomes the brown smear of the bull.

*Better path.* Painted muslin or canvas backdrop with mottled brown tones, scuffed and stained. Closer to dirt-and-shadow than paper.

*Best path.* Actual location with brown architecture. Tan stucco wall, unfinished concrete shop, stable, horse arena in Burbank or Sylmar. Adds production complexity but solves the backdrop problem in one move. Worth a half-day of scouting before committing to studio.

**Lighting.** This is the discipline check. Available or continuous only. No strobe. A single LED panel or a north-facing window in studio. Outdoors, late afternoon sun bouncing off a wall, no reflectors, no scrims.

The aesthetic statement says place a rim before the key. On this frame, do the opposite. No rim. No key separation. Let the figure live inside the field instead of standing forward of it. That's the refusal-of-light borrow the study named for personal work.

If a clean blur can't be had without strobe, the hedge is rear-curtain sync at 1/8 second with the strobe at low power so the trail dominates and the freeze is a faint anchor. Use it only if the pure-ambient version fails after a real attempt. Don't lead with the hedge.

**Gear.**

- SNIPED body.
- 50mm prime. 35mm if the room is tight.
- Tripod, locked off. Hand shake at 1/8 turns the whole picture into mush. The *figure* should blur, not the *frame*.
- ND filter (3-stop or 6-stop) if shooting in daylight. Otherwise can't get to 1/15 at f/4.
- Cable release or 2-second timer to kill shutter shake.
- Continuous LED panel if studio.

What I don't bring: strobes (unless hedge), softboxes, V-flats, beauty dish, stylist, second body, three lens options, smoke. Production restraint is part of the assignment.

**Direction protocol on the day.** Frame the camera with no subject in it first. Lock the composition. Decide where the figure enters and exits the frame. Mark the floor. Tell the dancer the move (a sweep, a turn, a cape pass equivalent) and have them do it ten times so the muscle memory is set. Then start shooting at 1/15.

Don't watch the LCD between takes for the first 30 frames. Just shoot the run. Chimp at frame 30, adjust shutter or framing, run another 30. Keep running until the dancer is tired or the frame is in the can. The frame will probably be one of frames 47 through 80, never one of the first ten.

## 4. The post

Carousel, same protocol as Frank. Side-by-side flattens this into a comparison and the recreation always loses to the original in a comparison because the original has 70 years of context behind it.

- Slide 1: my version, full frame. No text on image.
- Slide 2: the original. Credit on bottom corner: "Ernst Haas, *Bullfight, Spain*, 1956."
- Slide 3: detail crop from my frame. The hand, the cape sweep, whatever moment inside the blur reads strongest.
- Slide 4: BTS or wider scene if I have one. The setup, the dancer mid-movement, an alt frame.
- Slide 5: text slide with the lesson.

### Caption template

> Frame 9 of the Art Series. Ernst Haas, *Bullfight, Spain*, 1956.
>
> Haas dragged the shutter to 1/15 in the bullring at Madrid and let the matador dissolve into a streak of red and gold against the brown smear of the bull. The picture isn't a record of what happened. It's a record of what motion felt like.
>
> Recreating this meant deliberately giving up the control my work runs on. No rim light. No strobe. No directed pose. Locked the palette, set the conditions, slowed the shutter, and let the body do whatever it did. Anti-everything the SNIPED frame is built on, which is exactly why this one is ninth in the series.
>
> What I learned: [one sentence, written after the shoot]
>
> Original: Ernst Haas, *Bullfight, Spain*, 1956. Kodachrome.

Don't write the "what I learned" line in advance. The whole reason Haas is Frame 9 is to teach something the first eight frames couldn't. If the lesson can be written before the shoot, surrender didn't actually happen on set, and the post is dishonest. Write it after edit.

## 5. Timeline

Two weeks is realistic if casting starts now. One week is possible only if the dancer is already known.

**Days 1 to 2.** Casting. Reach out to three to five dancers. Lock one. Confirm rate, date, two-hour call. Same days, source the wardrobe, all red. Thrift first (Jet Rag, Crossroads Silver Lake), retail backup (Zara, Aritzia, COS). Decide on the gold accent piece.

**Days 3 to 4.** Location lock. Half day scouting if going stable or stucco. Otherwise order the brown seamless paper. Test the ND filter on a different subject in the apartment to confirm 1/15 is hand-shake-clean on the tripod. Unsexy test that prevents a wasted shoot day.

**Day 5 or 6.** Production. Two-hour call. Plan to overshoot. Aim for 200 frames minimum. Don't review on set past the first chimp.

**Days 7 to 9.** Edit. Cull to 20, then 5, then 1. Edit the one. Pull the SNIPED preset back hard, or build a Haas-specific develop preset that lives at 30 percent of the usual edit. Round trip to Evoto only if skin needs it, which on a blur frame it usually doesn't.

**Day 10.** Write the "what I learned" line. Build the carousel. Schedule the post.

**Days 11 to 14.** Post. Buffer for re-shoot if the first attempt didn't hit. On this frame specifically expect maybe 30 percent odds of needing a second pass. Slow-shutter math looks fine on the LCD and falls apart at 100 percent on the laptop. Build the buffer in. Don't post a frame I'm not actually behind because this one sits in the series for the rest of the project.

---

The actual hard part of this shoot isn't gear or wardrobe. It's standing on set and not reaching for the rim light when the LCD isn't showing what I want. That's the whole assignment. Gear it light enough that the temptation isn't even available.
